\section{Introduction}

Every result reported in Chapters~\ref{chp:synthetic} and~\ref{chp:attribution} was produced by a
single simulator. The training split, the validation split and the held-out test split were all
generated by the same code, from the same signal model, with the same clutter statistics. They
differ only in their random seeds and in the scenarios they sample. A held-out split constructed
this way establishes that the detector generalises to \emph{new samples} of the domain it was
developed on; it cannot establish that the detector generalises to a \emph{different} domain. That
distinction is the difference between sampling generalisation and external validity, and no
quantity of additional synthetic data can close it.

This matters because the comparison in Chapter~\ref{chp:synthetic} favoured the learned detector,
and because the attribution programme in Chapter~\ref{chp:attribution} located that advantage in
the learned decision rule rather than in any simple energy argument. Both conclusions are internal
to the simulator. If the simulator's background statistics differ materially from those of a real
sensor, a detector trained to exploit them may or may not retain its advantage --- and nothing in
the synthetic evidence indicates which.

This chapter therefore asks a single question:

\begin{quote}
Do the already-frozen TinyCNN and classical CFAR detectors transfer zero-shot from the synthetic
development domain to real automotive radar data?
\end{quote}

The word \emph{zero-shot} is load-bearing. Neither detector is retrained, fine-tuned, recalibrated
or re-thresholded against real data. The models evaluated here are the same checkpoints, with the
same weights, the same window geometry, the same calibrated thresholds and the same
non-maximum-suppression radius used in Chapter~\ref{chp:synthetic}. What changes is only the data
they are shown.

Two points of scope should be stated before any result appears. First, this is an
\textbf{external validation}, not a replacement for the synthetic benchmark: the two answer
different questions, and a poor real-data outcome would bound the external validity of the
synthetic findings rather than retract them. Second, the real and synthetic experiments do not
share a headline metric. The synthetic benchmark scores a point target within a $\pm 1$-bin gate at
a matched measured false-alarm rate; the real dataset annotates extended object regions and
supports no equivalent false-alarm measurement at all. Section~\ref{sec:prereg} makes that
difference explicit and fixes what may and may not be compared. Numbers from the two domains are
never pooled anywhere in this report.

The chapter proceeds in the order the work was carried out. Section~\ref{sec:discipline} describes
the experimental discipline adopted to keep the result credible, since a real-data experiment
performed after the synthetic results are known is unusually easy to bias.
Section~\ref{sec:carrada} introduces the dataset. Section~\ref{sec:r0} audits how far the two
domains actually differ, before any detector is run. Section~\ref{sec:prereg} states the
pre-registered evaluation protocol. Section~\ref{sec:r1} reports the one-shot result, and
Section~\ref{sec:crossdomain} places it beside the synthetic benchmark.

\section{Experimental discipline}
\label{sec:discipline}

A real-data evaluation carried out at the end of a project, by the person who produced the
synthetic result, with full knowledge of what that result was, is exposed to a specific failure
mode: the many small methodological choices that a new dataset forces --- what counts as ground
truth, what counts as a detection, which operating point to use, which detector variant to treat as
the baseline --- can each be settled, quite unconsciously, in the direction that flatters the
expected answer. The individual decisions are all defensible; their accumulation is not. Because
the outcome of this chapter contradicts the synthetic result, the protections against that failure
mode matter more, not less.

Four measures were adopted.

\paragraph{Isolation.} The real-data work was carried out in a self-contained experiment directory
that reads the frozen benchmark and writes nothing back to it. Before any real data was processed,
a manifest recorded the SHA-256 hash, size and protection category of \textbf{628 files}: the eight
frozen network checkpoints and their training configurations, the network, CFAR, evaluation and
radar source modules, the frozen configuration file, the calibrated threshold table, the dataset
configurations and caches, the headline result tables, the CFAR baseline outputs, and the complete
outputs of the three attribution experiments. That manifest was re-verified at the end of every
phase. No protected file changed at any point.

\paragraph{Pre-registration.} The complete evaluation protocol --- ground-truth definition,
eligibility rule, matching algorithm, primary and secondary metrics, statistical plan, and the
wording permitted when describing false alarms --- was written, hashed and committed
\emph{before any real detector output existed}. The frozen document has SHA-256
\texttt{e800ad84\ldots1bba98a8}. It was not edited afterwards.

\paragraph{One-shot evaluation.} The official test sequences were passed through the detectors
exactly once. The access event is recorded with its timestamp, the repository commit, the
pre-registration hash, the run-manifest hash, the environment, and the hash of every model
checkpoint used. Raw detector output was written to disk and hashed before any summary statistic
was computed, so every table and figure in this chapter is traceable to immutable evidence.

\paragraph{Separation of development from evaluation.} Implementation and debugging used the
CARRADA \emph{training} sequences only. The operating threshold required by the learned detector
was derived from \emph{synthetic} validation data alone, with a runtime file-access audit
confirming that no other dataset was opened during its derivation. The validation sequences were
never used, and the test sequences were touched only by the single scoring run.

trail, including the pre-registration text and the isolation manifest, is reproduced in
Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}.

could have been made to favour an outcome was fixed, in writing, before the outcome was known.

\section{The CARRADA dataset}
\label{sec:carrada}

CARRADA~\cite{ouaknine2021carrada} is a public dataset of synchronised camera and automotive radar
recordings, collected on a test track with a 77\,GHz frequency-modulated continuous-wave sensor and
released with annotations on both range--angle and range--Doppler representations. It was selected
for three reasons: it publishes range--Doppler data directly, so no re-processing from raw ADC
samples is required; its annotations are given in range--Doppler coordinates, so no projection step
is needed; and its published grid geometry matches the geometry this project's simulator was
configured to reproduce.

That last point requires care. The simulator was specified in Chapter~\ref{chp:simulator} to
reproduce CARRADA's \emph{published range--Doppler grid} --- map dimensions and bin spacings --- so
that a target occupies a comparable number of cells in both domains. It does not reproduce
CARRADA's waveform, its scenes, its clutter statistics or its sensor hardware.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Matching a grid is not matching a radar.} The geometric correspondence removes one
confound; it does not make the two domains equivalent, and Section~\ref{sec:r0} measures how far
apart they remain.
\end{quote}

\subsection{Contents and terminology}

The dataset comprises \CarradaSequences{} sequences and \CarradaFrames{} range--Doppler frames.
Four distinct quantities are easily conflated when describing it, and because they act as
denominators for every statistic reported later, they are fixed here
(Table~\ref{tab:carrada_terms}).

\begin{table}[h!]
\centering
\caption{CARRADA quantities, fixed to avoid the ambiguity that arises when all four are called
``instances''.}
\label{tab:carrada_terms}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrl@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Quantity} & \textbf{Count} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
\midrule
Total frames                  & \CarradaFrames          & frames with a range--Doppler array \\
Annotated frames              & \CarradaAnnotatedFrames & frames containing at least one object \\
Empty frames                  & \CarradaEmptyFrames     & frames with no annotated object \\
Frame-level object instances  & \CarradaObjectFrames    & summed over frames, objects present \\
Unique tracks                 & \CarradaTracks          & distinct physical objects \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The distinction between the last two rows is the one that matters. A single physical vehicle
tracked across two hundred frames contributes \emph{one} track but \emph{two hundred} frame-level
object instances. Detection statistics in this chapter are computed per frame-level object
instance; the track count indicates how much genuinely independent content the dataset holds, and
\CarradaTracks{} distinct objects across \CarradaSequences{} sequences is a small number. It is the
reason the statistical treatment in Section~\ref{sec:r1} takes the sequence, and not the frame or
the object, as its unit of analysis.

Objects belong to three classes --- pedestrian, cyclist and car --- and no frame contains more than
two annotated objects. The dataset therefore offers no counterpart to the multi-target,
mutual-masking and unresolved-pair conditions that Chapter~\ref{chp:synthetic} used to separate
detector behaviour; that limitation is revisited in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\subsection{Splits}

CARRADA publishes a sequence-level partition, reproduced in Table~\ref{tab:carrada_splits}, and
this work adopts it unchanged. Splitting instead by frame would be indefensible here: with only
\CarradaTracks{} tracks, consecutive frames of the same object are near-duplicates, and a random
frame-level split would place the same physical vehicle on both sides of the partition. The
reported test performance would then be closer to a memorisation check than to a generalisation
measurement.

\subsection{Data retained}

The full CARRADA distribution occupies approximately 96\,GB, the bulk of which is annotation masks
stored as dense floating-point arrays for every frame of every view. Since the entire annotation
content is also published in a single 37\,MB coordinate file, the redundancy was verified --- masks
reconstructed from the coordinate file were byte-identical to the stored arrays on 360 sampled
frame--annotation-type pairs, across all \CarradaSequences{} sequences --- and the redundant arrays
were removed, along with the range--angle and angle--Doppler views that this range--Doppler study
does not use. What remains is the range--Doppler data, the annotations, the metadata and a sample
of camera images, totalling 3.5\,GB.

Figure~\ref{fig:rd_domains} shows one frame from each domain, on identical axes and with identical
display normalisation. The visual differences it suggests --- a smoother background in the real
data, and a far narrower and more intense zero-Doppler ridge --- are quantified in the next
section. The figure is included to build intuition and is explicitly not evidence: it shows two
hand-picked frames, whereas Section~\ref{sec:r0} measures every frame in both domains.

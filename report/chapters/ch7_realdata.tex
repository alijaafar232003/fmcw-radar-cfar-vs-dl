\section{Introduction}

Every result in Chapters~\ref{chp:synthetic} and~\ref{chp:attribution} came from a single
simulator. Training, validation and held-out test splits were generated by the same code from the
same signal model, differing only in random seed and in the scenarios they sample. A held-out split
of that kind shows the detector generalises to \emph{new samples} of its own domain. It cannot show
that the detector generalises to a \emph{different} domain, and no quantity of additional synthetic
data can close that gap.

This matters because the synthetic comparison favoured the learned detector, and because the
attribution experiments located that advantage in the learned decision rule rather than in any
simple energy argument. Both conclusions are internal to the simulator. If a real sensor's
background statistics differ materially from the simulator's, a detector trained to exploit them
may or may not keep its advantage, and nothing in the synthetic evidence says which.

This chapter therefore asks a single question:

\begin{quote}
Do the already-frozen TinyCNN and classical CFAR detectors transfer zero-shot from the synthetic
development domain to real automotive radar data?
\end{quote}

The word \emph{zero-shot} is load-bearing. Neither detector is retrained, fine-tuned or recalibrated
against real data. The detector weights, the input preprocessing, the CFAR window geometry and the
final suppression radius all remain exactly as frozen in Chapter~\ref{chp:detectors}.

The operating threshold is the one thing that cannot simply carry over, and it is worth being
precise about why. Chapter~\ref{chp:synthetic} sets each detector's threshold by sweeping it per
condition and comparing the arms where their \emph{measured} false-alarm rates agree. That rule
needs labels on the data being scored, and CARRADA provides no false-alarm axis to sweep against.
For real data the threshold is therefore \textbf{fixed in advance: one value per frozen seed,
derived from synthetic validation data alone}, as Section~\ref{sec:prereg} sets out. No CARRADA
data of any split enters that derivation. The two chapters use different threshold rules, and their
headline numbers are never pooled.

One qualification belongs here rather than in a footnote. The evaluation below was run under a
post-processing protocol revised on synthetic development data after an earlier version of this
experiment. No detector changed and no real measurement informed the revision, so the result
remains zero-shot as defined above --- but it is a re-run with one field revised, not the original
untouched run. Appendix~\ref{app:prereg} states what changed and what did not.

Two points of scope. This is an \textbf{external validation}, not a replacement for the synthetic
benchmark: a poor real-data outcome would bound the external validity of the synthetic findings
rather than retract them. And the two experiments do not share a headline metric --- the synthetic
benchmark scores a point target within a $\pm 1$-bin gate at a matched measured false-alarm rate,
while the real dataset annotates extended regions and supports no false-alarm measurement at all.
Numbers from the two domains are never pooled anywhere in this report.

The chapter follows the order the work was done: experimental discipline
(Section~\ref{sec:discipline}), the dataset (Section~\ref{sec:carrada}), a domain audit run before
any detector (Section~\ref{sec:r0}), the evaluation protocol (Section~\ref{sec:prereg}), the result
(Section~\ref{sec:r1}), and the cross-domain comparison (Section~\ref{sec:crossdomain}).

\section{Experimental discipline}
\label{sec:discipline}

This evaluation was run at the end of the project, by the person who produced the synthetic result,
knowing what that result was. A new dataset forces many small choices --- what counts as ground
truth, what counts as a detection, which operating point to use, which CFAR variant is the baseline
--- and each could be settled, quite unconsciously, in the direction that flatters the expected
answer. Individually those decisions are defensible; together they are not.

Four measures guard against that. The audit trail behind them --- pre-registration hash, freeze
record, isolation manifest and commit chain --- is in Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}.

\paragraph{Isolation.} The real-data work ran in a self-contained directory that reads the frozen
benchmark and writes nothing back to it. Every protected file was hashed before any real data was
processed and re-verified after each phase. None changed.

\paragraph{Pre-registration.} The complete protocol --- ground truth, eligibility, matching,
metrics, statistical plan, and the wording permitted for unmatched detections --- was written,
hashed and committed \emph{before any real detector output existed}, and not edited afterwards.

\paragraph{One read, then one disclosed re-run.} The original evaluation scored the test sequences
exactly once under the pre-registered protocol. \textbf{The results below are not that first read.}
After the suppression radius was reselected on synthetic development data, the same sequences were
re-scored once under the revised radius-\ProtocolNms{} rule, and that re-run is what
Section~\ref{sec:r1} reports. It is a second read, made after the first result was known, and is
recorded as such. The original radius-\ProtocolNmsOriginal{} outputs were neither deleted nor
overwritten.

\paragraph{Development kept apart from evaluation.} Implementation and debugging used the CARRADA
\emph{training} sequences only. The learned detector's threshold came from \emph{synthetic}
validation data alone, confirmed by a runtime file-access audit, and the radius revision followed
the same rule: synthetic development splits only, no CARRADA data consulted. The CARRADA validation
sequences were never used, and the test sequences were read only by the two runs above.

None of this makes the result correct. It makes it \emph{auditable}: every choice that could have
favoured an outcome was fixed, in writing, before the outcome was known.

\section{The CARRADA dataset}
\label{sec:carrada}

CARRADA~\cite{ouaknine2021carrada} is a public dataset of synchronised camera and automotive radar
recordings, collected on a test track with a 77\,GHz frequency-modulated continuous-wave sensor and
released with annotations on both range--angle and range--Doppler representations. It was selected
for three reasons: it publishes range--Doppler data directly, so no re-processing from raw ADC
samples is required; its annotations are given in range--Doppler coordinates, so no projection step
is needed; and its published grid geometry matches the geometry this project's simulator was
configured to reproduce.

That last point requires care. The simulator was specified in Chapter~\ref{chp:simulator} to
reproduce CARRADA's \emph{published range--Doppler grid} --- map dimensions and bin spacings --- so
that a target occupies a comparable number of cells in both domains. It does not reproduce
CARRADA's waveform, its scenes, its clutter statistics or its sensor hardware.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Matching a grid is not matching a radar.} The geometric correspondence removes one
confound; it does not make the two domains equivalent, and Section~\ref{sec:r0} measures how far
apart they remain.
\end{quote}

denominators for every statistic reported later, they are fixed here

two annotated objects. The dataset therefore offers no counterpart to the multi-target,
mutual-masking and unresolved-pair conditions that Chapter~\ref{chp:synthetic} used to separate
detector behaviour; that limitation is revisited in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\subsection{Data retained}

The full CARRADA distribution occupies about 96\,GB, mostly annotation masks stored as dense arrays
for every frame of every view. The same annotation content is also published as a compact
coordinate file. That redundancy was verified by reconstructing masks from the coordinates and
comparing them against the stored arrays (Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}), after which the redundant

does not use. What remains --- range--Doppler data, annotations, metadata and a sample of camera
images --- totals 3.5\,GB.

\section{Domain audit}
\label{sec:r0}

Before either detector was run on real data, the two domains were compared directly. The purpose
was to establish, independently and in advance, how far apart they are --- so that whatever the
detectors then did could be interpreted against a measured baseline rather than an assumption. No
detector was involved in any measurement in this section.

\subsection{Representation}

CARRADA publishes each range--Doppler frame in three forms, and choosing between them is the first
substantive decision. Regressing the log of one against another across 240 frames spanning all
\CarradaSequences{} sequences gives a slope of \RzeroSlope{} $\pm$ \RzeroSlopeSD{} --- that is,
exactly a factor of two --- with a residual standard deviation of \RzeroResidual{}\,dB showing no
dependence on signal level or on position in the map. A slope of two in this relation is the
signature of a $20\log_{10}$ transform, which identifies one array as an amplitude and the other as
a power expressed in decibels.

Two consequences follow, and both matter because they let the frozen detectors be fed real data
without modification.

\textbf{CFAR needs linear power}, obtained from the published decibel array $D$ as
%
\begin{equation}
P \;\propto\; 10^{\,D/10}.
\label{eq:cfar_input}
\end{equation}
%
CFAR is a ratio test, so the unknown constant relating $P$ to absolute power cancels exactly and
never has to be determined. Scaling the input over eight orders of magnitude leaves the CFAR
statistic unchanged and produces identical peak sets.

\textbf{The network's preprocessing cancels the same constant.} Its frozen transform is
$10\log_{10}(P/\mathrm{median}\,P)$. The decibel transform is monotone, so
$\mathrm{median}(P) = 10^{\,\mathrm{median}(D)/10}$ exactly and the transform reduces to
%
\begin{equation}
x \;=\; \operatorname{clip}\!\left(D - \operatorname{median} D,\; -10,\; +60\right) / 20 ,
\label{eq:cnn_input}
\end{equation}
%
in which the scale constant again drops out. In practice the frozen preprocessing code is called
unchanged, fed $10^{\,D/20}$, and the two paths agree to within single-precision rounding.

\textbf{No detector-specific preprocessing was redesigned for real data.} Both arms run the code
they were frozen with. Appendix~\ref{app:prereg} records the numerical agreement in both checks.

\subsection{Geometry}

The grid transfers exactly. Every one of the \CarradaFrames{} frames is a $256 \times 64$ array,
matching the simulator's map dimensions, and the annotation coordinates index that array directly:
across all annotated cells the range indices span 0--255 and the Doppler indices 0--63. No
resampling, interpolation or coordinate transformation is required anywhere in the pipeline. This
is by construction --- the simulator was specified against CARRADA's published grid --- but it is
worth confirming, because it removes the most common source of silent error when transferring a
detector between datasets.

\subsection{Background statistics}
\label{sec:r0_background}

Comparing background statistics requires deciding which cells count as background, and that
decision is easy to get wrong in ways that flatter or damage either domain. A single definition
was therefore fixed and applied identically to both. A cell is eligible background if it survives
three exclusions: annotated object support dilated by three bins in each direction, so that target
skirts do not leak in; a band of $\pm 4$ Doppler bins about zero, since the ridge there is
structure rather than speckle; and the outer border that a $21 \times 9$ CFAR window cannot test,
leaving the same $236 \times 56 = 13{,}216$ cells the detectors themselves see.

No percentile trimming is applied at any point. This is stated explicitly because an early version
of this analysis did trim the upper decile, which biases the coefficient of variation downward and
produced a materially different answer; that version is superseded and none of its numbers appear
in this report.

Table~\ref{tab:r0_stats} reports the result. Uncertainty is taken across groups --- the
\CarradaSequences{} CARRADA sequences and the eight simulator scenario conditions --- never across
frames, because frames within a sequence are strongly correlated and treating millions of cells as
independent would produce meaninglessly narrow intervals.

Three readings follow.

\paragraph{The simulator behaves exactly as designed.} Its background has $k = \RzeroSimGammaK{}$
against a theoretical value of 1.0 for single-look exponential speckle, and a decibel spread of
\RzeroSimDBstd{}\,dB against a theoretical 5.57\,dB. This is an independent validation of the
clutter model developed in Chapter~\ref{chp:simulator}, obtained as a by-product of building a
comparison, and it holds uniformly across range.

\paragraph{CARRADA is markedly smoother.} Its effective Gamma shape is
$k = \RzeroCarradaGammaK{}$ and its decibel spread \RzeroCarradaDBstd{}\,dB, less than half the
simulator's. The wording here is deliberate: the measurement licenses the statement that
\emph{CARRADA background statistics are consistent with an effective Gamma shape near
\RzeroCarradaGammaK{}}. It does not license the claim that the sensor performs some number of
independent looks, which the dataset authors document nowhere.

\paragraph{Smoother does not mean cleaner.} Lower cell-to-cell fluctuation is not less clutter. The
same audit finds CARRADA carrying far stronger \emph{localised} structure --- most obviously a
zero-Doppler ridge of \RzeroCarradaRidge{}\,dB with a half-width of only
$\pm\RzeroCarradaRidgeHalf{}$ bin, against the simulator's broader and weaker
\RzeroSimRidge{}\,dB spread over $\pm\RzeroSimRidgeHalf{}$ bins. The real domain is therefore
\emph{different}, not simply easier or harder: a flatter field interrupted by strong narrow
structure, rather than a uniformly grainy one.

One further property is recorded because it shapes the interpretation of the results later.
CARRADA's background is \textbf{not stationary in range}: measured over the same fixed blocks used
throughout this chapter, its effective Gamma shape varies by roughly a factor of two and a half
between the nearest and the middle blocks. The simulator's is flat across every block. A detector
tuned on a stationary background therefore meets, in the real domain, a field whose statistics
change with range.

\subsection{Object support}

The final difference is the one with the largest consequence for measurement. A synthetic target
occupies a single true cell, and Chapter~\ref{chp:detectors} scores detections against it with a
$\pm 1$-bin gate. CARRADA annotates \emph{extended object support}: a segmentation region covering
tens of cells, whose median extent is 18 $\times$ 13 bins for a car, 11 $\times$ 11 for a cyclist
and 9 $\times$ 9 for a pedestrian (Figure~\ref{fig:r0_footprint}).

\begin{figure}[h!]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/Chapter7/r0_fig6_object_footprint.png}
\caption[Annotated object support by class]{Extent of the annotated dense support by class,
measured over all \CarradaObjectFrames{} frame-level object instances. A synthetic point target
occupies one bin.}
\label{fig:r0_footprint}
\end{figure}

A related trap is worth recording. CARRADA also publishes a bounding box per object, and it is
tempting to treat it as the extent of the dense mask. It is not: the box is strictly smaller than
the dense support on both axes in \CarradaObjectFrames{} of \CarradaObjectFrames{} instances, so
the two describe different annotation constructs. Quoting one while meaning the other would
understate object extent by roughly a quarter.

The consequence is that the project's synthetic truth definition cannot transfer. There is no point
target to match within $\pm 1$ bin, and inventing one --- by taking a centroid, a box centre or the
brightest cell inside the mask --- would mean scoring the detectors against a construct of this
project rather than an annotation the dataset provides. Section~\ref{sec:prereg} settles that
question before any result exists.

\section{Real-data evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:prereg}

The domain audit showed that the synthetic metric cannot transfer, so the evaluation had to be
redesigned for real data. Redesigning a metric with the results in view is exactly where
unconscious bias operates, so the protocol was written in full, hashed and committed before any real
detector output existed (Section~\ref{sec:discipline}). Table~\ref{tab:prereg} summarises what it
fixes.

\begin{table}[h!]
\centering
\caption{The pre-registered evaluation protocol, fixed before any real detector output existed.}
\label{tab:prereg}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.28\textwidth}p{0.63\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Element} & \textbf{Decision} \\
\midrule
Ground truth & The dense segmentation support. No point target is invented; centroid, box centre,
brightest cell and sparse centroid are all explicitly rejected. \\
Eligible region & The frozen $21\times 9$ detector-valid interior, unchanged from
Chapter~\ref{chp:detectors}. \\
Eligible object & One whose support intersects that region in at least one cell. \\
Matching & One-to-one, maximum-cardinality, identical code for every detector arm; ties broken on
detector score, then on a deterministic coordinate ordering. \\
Primary metric & Exact dense-mask object recall: the fraction of eligible objects for which an
assigned peak lies inside the annotated support. \\
Tolerance analysis & The same recall with the mask dilated by $\pm 1$ and $\pm 3$ bins. \\
Localisation & \textbf{Not reported.} CARRADA supplies no point truth, and distance-to-mask is
identically zero for any success. \\
False alarms & \textbf{Not reported as $P_{fa}$}, in any form. \\
Burden metric & Unmatched detections per frame. \\
Statistics & Paired bootstrap over the \RoneNseq{} test sequences, 10{,}000 replicates. Seed
variability reported separately and never merged with sequence variability. \\
Primary classical arm & CA-CFAR. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Two of these deserve explanation, since they are the points on which this evaluation departs most
sharply from Chapter~\ref{chp:synthetic}.

\paragraph{Why no false-alarm rate is reported.} A false-alarm measurement requires knowing that a
detection corresponds to nothing real. CARRADA annotates only its \CarradaTracks{} tracked road
users; it does not annotate the buildings, parked vehicles, poles and roadside structure that a
real scene contains, and the domain audit found bright unannotated returns in a measurable fraction
of frames. A peak outside every annotated mask may therefore be infrastructure, static clutter,
multipath, an unannotated object --- or a genuine false alarm, with no way to distinguish them.
Reporting such counts as $P_{fa}$ would assert knowledge the dataset does not contain. They are
reported instead as \emph{unmatched detections per frame}, an output-density measure, and that
term is used consistently throughout.

\paragraph{Why the operating point is a new protocol.} The two domains use different threshold
rules, and the difference is worth stating precisely because it is the main reason their headline
numbers cannot be pooled.

\begin{description}
\itemsep2pt
\item[Chapter~\ref{chp:synthetic}] sweeps each detector's score threshold \emph{per condition} and
compares the arms where their measured false-alarm rates agree. This is an \textbf{evaluation
operating point}, not a deployable setting: it needs labels on the very data being scored, and it
would differ from condition to condition in service. Its purpose is to remove threshold choice as a
confound from the comparison.
\item[This chapter] cannot use that rule --- CARRADA supplies no false-alarm axis to sweep against.
A \textbf{single global threshold} was therefore derived per frozen seed from \emph{synthetic
validation data alone}, pooled across all eight scenario conditions at a measured per-cell
false-alarm rate of $10^{-4}$, and then applied unchanged to every real frame. \textbf{No CARRADA
data of any split entered that derivation}, and a runtime file-access audit confirms it.
\end{description}

This is the harder requirement of the two: one fixed threshold, chosen without sight of the data it
will run on. The consequence, registered in advance, is that R1 operates under a different
operating-point protocol from Chapter~\ref{chp:synthetic}, and its numbers are therefore
\textbf{not poolable} with the synthetic tables.

\section{Zero-shot transfer}
\label{sec:r1}

The results below come from the revised-protocol re-run described in
Section~\ref{sec:discipline}: the official test sequences were re-scored once under the
radius-\ProtocolNms{} rule, with every other quantity above already fixed and unchanged from the
pre-registration. All eight frozen network seeds and all four CFAR variants were evaluated on the
same \CarradaTestFrames{} frames.

\subsection{Primary result}

Of the \CarradaTestObjects{} object instances in the test split, \RoneEligibleObjects{} remain
eligible once the detector-valid interior of Section~\ref{sec:prereg} is applied; the difference is
a single instance whose support lies entirely in the excluded border.

Two numbers describe the gap and it is worth separating them. \textbf{The raw pooled recall
difference is \RonePooledDelta{}}, obtained by pooling all eligible objects.
\textbf{The pre-registered sequence-balanced paired estimate, used for inference, is
\RoneDelta{}}, which weights each test sequence equally so that the longest sequence cannot
dominate. The two differ slightly because the sequences vary in length. Everything that follows
uses the sequence-balanced figure for inference and names the pooled figure explicitly where it is
meant.

\textbf{At exact annotated support the two arms are statistically indistinguishable.} The
sequence-balanced difference of \RoneDelta{} carries a \SI{95}{\percent} confidence interval of
$[\RoneCIlo,\ \RoneCIhi]$, which \emph{includes zero}. The point estimate favours the classical
detector and CA-CFAR is ahead in \RoneSeqCFARLead{} of the \RoneNseq{} test sequences, but the
margins are small and the network leads in the remaining one. The interval does not license a claim
that either arm wins. The synthetic ordering is not reproduced on real data --- and it is not
cleanly reversed either. It disappears. Figure~\ref{fig:r1_primary} shows the same comparison with the
individual seeds overlaid, and per test sequence.

The secondary CFAR variants are ahead of both: GO, SO and OS reach \RoneCFARGO{}, \RoneCFARSO{} and
\RoneCFAROS{} respectively. That ordering must be read together with
Section~\ref{sec:burden}, because those same variants also emit two and a half times CA-CFAR's
output; recall on this dataset is not free, and the arms are not separable on recall alone. CA
remains the primary comparator as pre-registered; no variant was promoted after the fact.

\paragraph{Zero Doppler.} \RoneNearZeroFrac{} of eligible test objects have support intersecting the

broader. \textbf{It does not.} Within that stratum the network reaches \RoneNearZeroTinyCNN{}

The ridge itself remains a real and measured difference between the domains --- the audit of
Section~\ref{sec:r0} established that before any detector was run --- but it is not the location of
a detection deficit, because on this evaluation there is no substantial deficit to locate.

\paragraph{Class.} Both detectors rank the classes in the same order, and it is the order of mask
size. This is a confound, not an incidental caveat: a larger annotated support is a larger area in
which a peak can land and be counted correct, so per-class recall cannot be read as class
difficulty alone. The two arms are close on cars and pedestrians, and the network is marginally
ahead on cyclists --- but that figure rests on only \RoneCycN{} instances drawn from two sequences
and is treated as exploratory.

\paragraph{Range.} The pattern is not monotone, and it is the one stratification where the arms
genuinely diverge. The network is behind in the nearest block (\RoneRangeATinyCNN{} against
\RoneRangeACFAR{}) and \emph{ahead} of CA-CFAR in the three middle blocks, by as much as
\RoneRangeDDiff{} at \RoneRangeDLabel{} bins. The near-range deficit is the largest single stratified
difference in the chapter, and it is consistent with the range non-stationarity recorded in
Section~\ref{sec:r0_background}: the simulator's background statistics are stationary in range,
CARRADA's are not, and the near field is where they differ most. A single pooled number would have
hidden both halves of this.

The network gains substantially more from dilation than the classical detector does, and the

Two cautions apply to that observation, and both matter.

First, \textbf{this is not a localisation measurement.} With extended truth there is no point
against which localisation could be measured; dilating a mask asks whether a peak fell
\emph{near} the annotated support, which is a different question from how precisely it was placed.
The synthetic localisation result of Section~\ref{sec:localization} is the localisation
measurement, and it runs the other way.

Second, a detector that emits more candidates has more chances to land one inside a dilated region.
Section~\ref{sec:burden} shows that the network does emit considerably more, so part of this gain
is bought rather than earned. The tolerance ladder and the output density have to be read together.

\textbf{This is the axis on which the two primary arms clearly differ, and it runs against the
network.} At their frozen operating points the network emits \RoneBurdenRatio{} times CA-CFAR's
unmatched output for statistically indistinguishable exact recall. Whatever the network gains at
the dilated gates of Table~\ref{tab:r1_tolerance}, it gains while producing roughly twice as much
output that no annotation accounts for.

The wider table also shows that this is not peculiar to the learned arm. Within the classical
family the same trade runs in the same direction: GO, SO and OS reach higher recall than CA and
emit two and a half times as much. \textbf{On this dataset recall and output density move together
for every detector tested}, which is precisely why recall alone cannot rank them.

Three reasons why the burden column must not be read as a false-alarm rate have already been given:
the annotation covers only tracked road users, the audit found bright unannotated returns, and the
suppression window is sized for point targets. The last point is measurable. A
$\ProtocolNmsWin \times \ProtocolNmsWin$ suppression window is far smaller than a median car's
$18 \times 13$ support, so one real object can legitimately produce several surviving peaks ---
which occurs for \RoneMultiPeakTinyCNN{} of objects for the network and \RoneMultiPeakCFARCA{} for
CA-CFAR. Under one-to-one matching exactly one of those peaks is matched and the remainder are
counted unmatched. A substantial part of the burden in Table~\ref{tab:r1_burden} is therefore an
artefact of applying a point-target suppression radius to extended truth, and it is common to both
arms.

One further check argues against reading the burden as a false-alarm rate. If the extra output were
generated by the annotated objects themselves, frames containing annotations would carry more of it
than frames containing none. They do not: the network averages \RoneBurdenTinyCNNAnn{} unmatched
detections on annotated frames against \RoneBurdenTinyCNNEmpty{} on annotation-empty ones, and
CA-CFAR \RoneBurdenCFARCAAnn{} against \RoneBurdenCFARCAEmpty{}. The output density is a property of
the scene, not of the annotation --- which is exactly why CARRADA cannot price it.

\subsection{Seed variability}

The eight frozen seeds span \RoneSeedMin{} to \RoneSeedMax{}, a standard deviation of
\RoneSeedSD{} (Figure~\ref{fig:r1_tolerance}). \RoneSeedsAboveCFAR{} of the eight exceed CA-CFAR's
\RoneCFARCA{} and the rest fall below it, which is what a genuinely close comparison looks like at
the seed level. No seed was selected, dropped or ensembled at any point.

Two things are worth reading from that spread. It is small in absolute terms --- comparable to the
paired difference the chapter is measuring --- so the pooled result is not an average over wildly
different networks. But it is not negligible either: eight networks with near-identical synthetic
development performance still differ measurably from best to worst under domain shift, and a
single-seed result would have been reported as a win or a loss depending only on which seed
happened to be trained. That is an argument for reporting seed populations rather than seeds, and
it is why every network value in this report is a mean over \TinySeeds{}.

This is reported as a property of this detector and this training configuration. It is not evidence
about neural networks in general, and one architecture on one dataset cannot support that claim.

\section{Cross-domain synthesis}
\label{sec:crossdomain}

Figure~\ref{fig:crossdomain} places the two domains side by side. Its construction reflects a
constraint stated throughout this chapter: the domains are shown as separate rows, each labelled
with its own metric, because synthetic $P_d$ and CARRADA mask recall measure different things and a
shared axis would imply otherwise. Within each row, the left panel shows levels and the right panel
shows the paired difference --- and it is the difference that carries meaning across the rows,
because within a domain it is a like-for-like contrast between two detectors on identical data.

\centering

The right-hand column carries the result. The synthetic benchmark showed \SynWins{} established
challenge-condition gains, a small non-significant difference on the unresolved stress test, and a
practical tie in homogeneous clutter, with the largest margin reaching \SynClutterEdgeDiff{} at
clutter edges. In the unseen real domain the sequence-balanced difference is \RoneDelta{}, with a
confidence interval that includes zero.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The learned advantage does not reverse on real data; it disappears.}
\end{quote}

That distinction carries weight, because a reversal and a vanished margin support different
conclusions. A reversal would say the classical detector is better on real data, and the evidence
does not show that. What it shows is narrower: \emph{the learned detector's measured advantage was
specific to its training domain}. Outside that domain the two arms are hard to separate on recall,
and easy to separate on how much output they emit to reach it.

Figure~\ref{fig:rd_examples} shows what the two domains look like at the level of individual
frames, with both detectors' outputs overlaid. Frames were chosen deterministically on annotation
geometry alone, never on which detector performed better.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/Chapter7/r1_fig6_rd_examples.png}
\caption[Range--Doppler examples with detector outputs]{Synthetic frames (top) and real CARRADA
frames (bottom) under one display convention, with the annotated truth and both detectors' peaks.

Set beside the domain audit, one point stands out. The audit identified the zero-Doppler ridge as
the largest single structural difference between the domains, yet that stratum shows no meaningful
separation between the detectors. The clearest separation is elsewhere, in the nearest range block,
where the audit measured range non-stationarity. \textbf{A large structural difference between
domains does not, on this evidence, translate mechanically into a detector difference.}

The chapter therefore supports a statement about \emph{sensitivity}: the learned detector's
advantage depended on properties of its training domain and did not transfer, while the classical
detector, whose threshold adapts to local statistics by construction, reached comparable recall
from a much sparser output. It does not support a causal attribution to any single measured
difference. Establishing that would need training on deliberately modified data --- a different
experiment, noted in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

\subsection{A tolerance asymmetry in both domains}

One pattern appears on both sides of the comparison. In the synthetic domain the frozen network
gains from a wider gate and loses at the exact cell: CA-CFAR reaches $P_d = 0.5$ at
\SynSnrFiftyExactCA{}\,dB on exact-cell scoring, while the frozen arm never reaches it, peaking at
\SynPdMaxExactDL{}. On real data the same shape appears in the tolerance ladder --- exact recall
\RoneTinyCNNExact{} rises to \RoneTinyCNNTolThree{} at three bins, a far larger gain than
CA-CFAR's.

A broader detector response is consistent with both, and may contribute to both. It is not
established as the cause of either. On the real-data side it is certainly not the whole story:
Section~\ref{sec:burden} shows the network emits \RoneBurdenRatio{} times CA-CFAR's output, and a
denser candidate set has more chances to land inside a dilated region whatever the shape of the
underlying response. Separating response breadth from output density would need an intervention
that was not performed.

Two further qualifications. On synthetic point targets a broad response is unambiguously a cost,
because the truth is one cell; on extended real objects it is not clearly either way, because the
truth is a region. And the breadth itself belongs to this frozen arm's $3\times3$ block label,
which Chapter~\ref{chp:attribution} shows is largely removable, not to learned detection as such.

\subsection{Post-processing matters more on extended objects}

A methodological lesson emerged that the chapter did not set out to find. This comparison is
sensitive not only to the two score maps but to the fixed rule converting a dense score map into
object candidates. The synthetic benchmark never exercises that sensitivity: its targets are points,
and any reasonable suppression window holds one maximum per target. Real annotated objects are
\emph{larger} than the suppression window, so the same rule operates \emph{inside} a single object.
The ranking of two detectors on real extended-object data therefore depends on that post-processing
choice as well as on the detectors. Appendix~\ref{app:prereg} records how this was found.

\section{Comparison with published results}
\label{sec:litcompare}

A result of this kind is only interpretable against what the field has already measured. This
section places both halves of the project --- the synthetic margin of
Chapter~\ref{chp:synthetic} and the transfer result just reported --- beside the published
literature. The purpose is calibration, not advocacy: the question is whether the numbers obtained
here are of a normal size, and whether the loss of the synthetic margin under domain shift is
larger than a reader should expect.

Section~\ref{sec:sota} already separated the corpus into families and established that only one
of them --- per-cell learned thresholding scored against CFAR at a matched false-alarm rate ---
measures the same quantity as this work. Only that family is used for numerical comparison here;
the others are cited where they inform interpretation and are never placed on a shared axis with
results from this work.

\subsection{The synthetic margin against the literature}

The comparable works report their margin in decibels of cell SNR at a fixed false-alarm rate.
Table~\ref{tab:lit_db} converts the synthetic benchmark of Chapter~\ref{chp:synthetic} into that
currency. At the $\pm 1$-bin gate and matched \emph{measured} $P_{fa}$ of $10^{-4}$, the frozen
network reaches $P_d = 0.5$ at \SynSnrFiftyDL{}\,dB against CA-CFAR's \SynSnrFiftyCA{}\,dB --- a
margin of \SynSnrGainDL{}\,dB.

Two observations follow, and they point in opposite directions.

The first is corroboration. The clutter-edge margin here, \SynClutterEdgeDiff{} in $P_d$, is
qualitatively consistent with and similar in magnitude to the $+0.25$ to $+0.35$ band Gusland
et al.\ report for clutter transitions on real X-band data, measured with a different architecture,
waveform and target class. The homogeneous-clutter tie (\SynClutterDiff{}, not significant) is
likewise consistent with RadCNN's noise-only scenario and Gusland's sub-450\,m regime. The overall
\SynSnrGainDL{}\,dB is of the same order as RadCNN's $+0.5$\,dB average, and was measured under a
stricter condition: RadCNN matches $P_{fa}$ by \emph{design} and reports its CFAR arms drifting to
between $1.2$ and $1.7 \times 10^{-3}$ against a design value of $10^{-3}$, whereas the arms here
are matched at measured $P_{fa}$.

The second observation is less comfortable. Every comparable work reports a condition in which its
network loses. RadCNN loses $0.8$\,dB when the target sits near clutter but the reference window is
mostly noise; Banerjee's detector falls below 80\,\% $P_d$ under strong interference on quantised
hardware. The synthetic benchmark of Chapter~\ref{chp:synthetic} contains no such condition: the
network establishes gains on five challenge conditions and is level on the other two, and never
loses. The honest reading is not that this network is better than published ones, but that the
synthetic suite lacks the condition that would expose it. RadCNN's losing scenario --- target in
noise with clutter intruding into the reference window --- has no counterpart among the seven
conditions evaluated here, and constructing one would be a direct extension of this work.

A third quantity bears on a disagreement in the literature about \emph{why} a learned detector wins
in homogeneous conditions. Lallouet et al.\ attribute it to CFAR loss: the threshold is estimated
from a finite reference window, so it is itself a random variable and the detector pays for that.
The sweep here measures it directly. At the exact-cell gate CA-CFAR reaches $P_d = 0.5$ at
\SynSnrFiftyExactCA{}\,dB against a clairvoyant bound of \SynSnrTheory{}\,dB --- a measured CFAR
loss of \SynCfarLossCA{}\,dB. That is real, and of the right order to matter near the knee of the
curve, but it is smaller than the \SynSnrGainDL{}\,dB margin, so it accounts for part of that
margin and not all of it. Chapter~\ref{chp:attribution} pursues the remainder.

The transfer result therefore cannot be placed beside a published transfer number. The one adjacent
observation in the wider corpus points the same way: Tosi et al.~\cite{tosi2025benchmarking} train
a CNN peak detector on a linearised signal model, evaluate it on data containing hardware
nonlinearities, and find it comparable to CFAR within its training regime and degrading outside it
--- attributing that to the coverage of the training distribution rather than to the architecture.
That is the same failure mode, observed independently on a different problem.

A second calibration is available, though it must be handled carefully. DAROD, a
$3.4$-million-parameter detector trained on the CARRADA training split for 50 epochs, reports a
recall of $46.03\,\%$ at an IoU threshold of $0.5$, and $52.52\,\%$ at $0.3$, on the same test
split used here. \textbf{That number is not comparable with the \RoneTinyCNN{} of
Table~\ref{tab:r1_primary}}: DAROD's metric requires a predicted bounding box to overlap the
annotation by a fixed fraction \emph{and} to carry the correct class label, whereas the metric used
here asks only whether a detected peak falls inside the annotated support. The metric used here is
by a wide margin the more permissive of the two, and no ranking between the two systems is claimed
or implied. The comparison is admissible for one purpose only: it establishes that CARRADA is a
demanding benchmark, on which a purpose-built supervised detector trained on the dataset itself
still misses roughly half of the annotated objects under its own criterion. Read against that, the
result reported in this chapter --- a simulator-trained network reaching recall comparable to a
classical detector on a domain it has never seen --- reads as a reasonable zero-shot outcome rather
than as evidence of a defective network.

\subsection{What this comparison licenses}

Three statements are supported.

\begin{enumerate}
\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{The synthetic margin is of a normal size.} At \SynSnrGainDL{}\,dB it sits alongside
RadCNN's $+0.5$\,dB, and the clutter-edge margin is similar in magnitude to the band Gusland
et al.\ measured independently on real data. Nothing in the synthetic result requires special
explanation.
\item \textbf{The transfer result is of the expected shape.} A detector that gains roughly a decibel
within its own domain and loses that margin under a change of data-generating process is behaving
as the one adjacent published observation predicts.
\item \textbf{The evaluation is stricter than the family standard} in three specific respects:
matching at measured rather than design $P_{fa}$; reporting a tolerance ladder rather than a single
gate; and evaluating on a domain never seen in training.
\end{enumerate}

Two statements are \emph{not} supported, and are recorded here so that the summary in the General
Conclusion cannot be read more broadly than the evidence allows. First, none of these comparisons
establishes that the detector built here outperforms any published detector. The systems differ in
architecture, data, waveform, metric and operating point, and no controlled comparison between them
was run; the tables above compare \emph{margins over a classical baseline}, not detectors. Second,
no directly comparable zero-shot result was identified in the reviewed corpus, so the transfer
result reported here has no external benchmark against which to calibrate it. A single measurement
is not a norm.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

Five limitations bound what this chapter can claim.

\begin{enumerate}
\itemsep4pt

\item \textbf{Dataset scope.} CARRADA is one sensor on one test track, and the effective sample
size for inference is \RoneNseq{} test sequences. That is small: the confidence interval is wide
because six sequences is not many, and a second dataset could reinforce or overturn this result.

\item \textbf{Ground truth and metric mismatch.} The primary metric asks whether a peak lands
inside an extended annotated region. It is not the synthetic point-target $P_d$, and the two are
never pooled. Class sizes and mask extents are also unbalanced --- the most numerous class has the
smallest masks --- so per-class recall cannot be read as class difficulty alone.

\item \textbf{No false-alarm measurement exists.} The comparison is one-sided: it measures what
each detector finds, not what it invents. Unmatched detections are an output-density measure and
not a false-alarm rate. This bounds the chapter's central trade-off rather than resolving it, since
the network's larger output cannot be priced against its recall on this dataset.

\item \textbf{Post-processing and data imbalance.} A point-target suppression radius applied to
extended objects inflates the unmatched burden for both arms, and the ranking measured here is
sensitive to that radius in a way the synthetic benchmark is not; a single radius is reported and
the dependence is documented. Separately, \RoneNearZeroFrac{} of test objects lie in the
zero-Doppler band, so the headline describes that regime more than it describes the map uniformly.

\item \textbf{Transfer scope.} No real-data training was performed \emph{in this chapter}, so its
result is a zero-shot bound and not a ceiling. Two follow-up experiments in
Chapter~\ref{chp:domaingap} test exactly what this bound leaves open --- first by rebuilding the
synthetic training domain from target-domain statistics, then by permitting real-domain
fine-tuning --- and both raise real-data recall well above the figure reported here. Nothing in this
chapter anticipated those results, and they are reported as follow-up work rather than folded back
into this evaluation. CFAR is also zero-shot here: its multipliers were calibrated for single-look
statistics, so its operating point on real data is not the designed one, and that it transferred
well is an observation rather than evidence its calibration was correct. Finally, this chapter
performs no causal intervention, so none of the measured domain differences can be said, on its
evidence alone, to have caused the result.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

This chapter asked whether the frozen detectors transfer zero-shot from the simulator to real
automotive radar data, and answered it under a protocol fixed in advance of the answer.

Both detectors transfer in the sense that both work: neither collapses on real measurements, and
both recall the large majority of annotated objects from a purely synthetic development process.
But the ordering established in Chapter~\ref{chp:synthetic} does not carry over. Frozen CA-CFAR
reaches \RoneCFARCA{} exact dense-mask object recall against the frozen network's \RoneTinyCNN{}, a
sequence-balanced paired difference of \RoneDelta{} whose confidence interval
$[\RoneCIlo, \RoneCIhi]$ includes zero. At exact annotated support the two arms are
\textbf{statistically indistinguishable}. The synthetic advantage is neither retained nor reversed;
it is absent.

Three qualifications complete that statement, and the third is the important one.

\textbf{The tolerance ladder favours the network.} Dilating the annotated support moves the
difference from \RoneDiffExact{} at exact support to \RoneDiffTolOne{} at $\pm 1$ bin and
\RoneDiffTolThree{} at $\pm 3$. More of the network's misses are near misses. This is tolerance
around an annotated region, not localisation accuracy, and a denser output has more chances to fall
inside a dilated one.

\textbf{The difference is not concentrated at zero Doppler.} The stratum that the domain audit had
identified as the largest structural difference between the domains shows a shortfall of
\RoneNearZeroTinyCNN{} against \RoneNearZeroCFAR{} --- essentially the same as the
\RoneAwayZeroTinyCNN{} against \RoneAwayZeroCFAR{} recorded clear of it. The ridge remains a
measured domain difference; it is not the site of a detection deficit. The clearest stratified
separation is elsewhere, in the nearest range block, where the network trails by
\RoneRangeADiff{}.

\textbf{Comparable recall is bought with substantially more output.} The network emits
\RoneBurdenTinyCNN{} unmatched detections per frame against CA-CFAR's \RoneBurdenCFARCA{} ---
\RoneBurdenRatio{} times as many. CARRADA supports no false-alarm measurement, so that cost cannot
be priced here, and it must not be described as a false-alarm rate; but it cannot be omitted from
any statement of the network's performance either. \textbf{The two arms are separable on output
density even where they are not separable on recall.}

None of this retracts the synthetic findings of Chapters~\ref{chp:synthetic}
and~\ref{chp:attribution}. Those were controlled experiments and they remain valid within the domain
that produced them. What this chapter establishes is the boundary of that validity: the learned
detector's measured advantage was specific to its training domain and did not transfer, while a
classical detector whose threshold adapts to local statistics by construction reached comparable
recall from a far sparser output. That is a narrower claim than the synthetic result alone would
have supported, and it is the claim the evidence sustains.

\textbf{The disappearance of the synthetic advantage shifts the question from detector capacity to
domain alignment.} The audit of Section~\ref{sec:r0} was run to decide whether this comparison was
fair, but it also measured something else: the simulator and the real sensor differ in background
smoothness, spread, ridge structure and range behaviour by margins far larger than any constant
tuned anywhere in this project. A detector whose decision rule is fixed by weights fitted to one of
those distributions was never guaranteed to carry to the other.

That turns a negative result into a testable hypothesis --- that the training distribution, not the
architecture, is what failed to transfer --- and it is the hypothesis Chapter~\ref{chp:domaingap}
sets out to test.

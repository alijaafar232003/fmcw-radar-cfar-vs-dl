\section{Introduction}

Chapter~\ref{chp:background} described detection in general terms. This chapter specifies the
particular radar, the particular simulator and the particular datasets that every result in this
report is measured on. All the numeric values deliberately withheld from Chapter~\ref{chp:background}
are introduced here.

Three decisions in this chapter shape everything that follows.

The first is the choice to work in simulation. It is what makes a controlled comparison possible at
all, because it supplies exact ground truth and independent control over each difficulty. It is
also the reason Chapter~\ref{chp:realdata} exists, since a result obtained entirely inside a
simulator says nothing on its own about real measurements.

The second is the sensor specification. The simulator reproduces the range--Doppler grid geometry
and bin spacing of the real dataset used later, which removes a basic resolution and scale
mismatch between the two domains. Without that, any transfer failure in
Chapter~\ref{chp:realdata} could be dismissed as a scaling artefact rather than a finding.
Section~\ref{sec:carrada_spec} records how those parameters were derived, including why the
bandwidth published alongside that dataset was not used directly.

The third is the dataset design. The benchmark is divided into predefined conditions rather than
generated entirely at random. This ensures that important cases such as clutter edges and target
masking occur often enough to evaluate the detectors reliably.

\section{Why simulate}

A comparison of the kind attempted here needs three things that measured radar data does not
readily provide.

\textbf{Exact ground truth.} Scoring $\Pd$ requires knowing precisely which cells contain targets.
Real datasets provide annotations, which are themselves estimates, produced semi-automatically and
carrying their own error. In simulation the target position is an input, not an inference.

\textbf{Independent control of difficulty.} The failure modes of
Section~\ref{sec:cfar_failures} --- clutter edges, target masking --- must be produced deliberately
and in isolation to be measured separately. In recorded data they co-occur uncontrollably.

\textbf{Sufficient quantity in rare conditions.} The conditions that discriminate between detectors
are exactly the ones that are rare in naturally sampled data.

The cost is external validity, and it is a real cost. A detector tuned inside one simulator may
have learned that simulator rather than radar. This is why the synthetic benchmark of
Chapter~\ref{chp:synthetic} is treated in this report as an internally valid measurement whose
generalisation is a separate question, tested in Chapter~\ref{chp:realdata} and not assumed here.

\section{Radar specification}
\label{sec:carrada_spec}

\subsection{Matching the real dataset's bin geometry}

The sensor parameters were chosen so that the simulator reproduces the range--Doppler grid geometry
and bin spacing of CARRADA~\cite{ouaknine2021carrada}, the real dataset used in
Chapter~\ref{chp:realdata}. The reason is that detectors operate in bins: a CFAR window is a fixed
number of cells wide, and a network's receptive field is a fixed number of cells across. If one
metre of range corresponded to five cells in one domain and two in the other, any difference
measured later would be partly a unit mismatch.

What this matching does and does not achieve should be stated clearly. It removes the geometric
confound, so that a given physical separation means the same number of cells in both domains. It
does \emph{not} make target responses identical. CARRADA contains extended road users whose energy
spreads over a region set by the object's physical size and aspect, whereas the simulator produces
point-target responses whose extent is set by the transform and its windows. Those two supports
differ, and Chapter~\ref{chp:realdata} treats that difference as one of its central issues rather
than assuming it away.

\subsection{Deriving a consistent effective bandwidth}
\label{sec:beff}

The bandwidth could not simply be copied from the dataset's published parameters, because the
published range figures do not agree with one another.

The published table gives 256 range bins over a maximum range of about \SI{50}{\metre}, which
corresponds to \SI{\CarradaBinSpacing}{\metre} per bin. The same table also lists a sweep bandwidth
of \SI{\CarradaQuotedB}{\giga\hertz}. Using the standard relation $\Delta R = c/2B$, that bandwidth
would imply a nominal range resolution of about \SI{0.0375}{\metre}, which is more than five times
finer than the published grid spacing. The two cannot both describe the same range axis.

Since the objective here is to reproduce the grid the dataset actually uses, the published grid
geometry was taken as the reference. Matching \SI{\CarradaBinSpacing}{\metre} per bin over
\SI{50}{\metre} requires an effective bandwidth of
\begin{equation}
B_{\text{eff}} = \frac{c \, N_{\text{bins}}}{2 R_{\max}} \approx \SI{\CarradaBeff}{\mega\hertz},
\label{eq:beff}
\end{equation}
which is the value adopted, rounded to \SI{\RadarB}{\mega\hertz}.

Two cross-checks support reading the published table this way.

First, the same table quotes an angular resolution of \ang{0.70}, while \ang{180} divided by 256
angle bins is \ang{0.703}. The entries labelled ``FFT resolution'' therefore appear to be bin
spacings rather than physical resolutions, and reading the range entry the same way makes it
self-consistent.

Second, the velocity axis is consistent throughout. The quoted velocity resolution implies a frame
time of \SI{4.635}{\milli\second}; divided by 64 chirps this gives a chirp interval of
\SI{\RadarTc}{\micro\second}, which reproduces the quoted maximum velocity to within
\SI{0.01}{\metre\per\second}. Only the range side requires interpretation.

One possible explanation for the \SI{\CarradaQuotedB}{\giga\hertz} figure is that it refers to the
configured radio-frequency sweep of the front end, of which only the portion falling inside the
sampled beat band contributes to the range transform. \textbf{This is an inference from the
published numbers, not something stated in the dataset documentation}, and nothing in this report
depends on it. What matters here is only that the simulator reproduces the grid geometry the
dataset actually exhibits.

Three points about this table are worth drawing out.

\textbf{Maximum range.} This is set by the Nyquist limit of the sampled beat band: a target at
\SI{\RadarRangeMax}{\metre} produces a beat frequency of exactly $f_s/2$.

\textbf{Maximum velocity.} A target moving faster than \SI{\RadarVelMax}{\metre\per\second}
radially does not disappear. Its Doppler shift falls outside the unambiguous interval and wraps
around into it, so the target still appears on the map but at an incorrect velocity. This is
Doppler ambiguity, and it is why the benchmark of Section~\ref{sec:datasets} draws velocities well
inside the unambiguous interval.

\textbf{Processing gain.} Combining \RadarN{} fast-time samples and \RadarM{} chirps concentrates a
target's coherent energy into a small region of the RD map while noise remains spread across it. In
the ideal, unwindowed case this concentration is worth \SI{\RadarCoherentGain}{\deci\bel}. The
range and Doppler window functions applied before each transform suppress spectral leakage but cost
some of that gain, leaving an effective \SI{\RadarProcGain}{\deci\bel}. The second figure is
computed directly from the window coefficients rather than measured from data, so it tracks any
change to the configuration automatically. It is the one used when setting dataset difficulty,
because it is the gain the simulated detector input actually receives.

\section{The simulator}

\subsection{Signal generation and processing}

The most important thing to understand about the simulator is what it does \emph{not} do: it never
draws a bright spot onto a range--Doppler map. Targets are specified in physical units, and the map
is produced by the same operations a real receiver performs.

\begin{enumerate}
\itemsep2pt
\item Each target is assigned a range $R$, a radial velocity $v$ and an amplitude representing its
reflectivity.
\item The transmitted chirp is synthesised as a linear frequency ramp.
\item The target's range evolves as $r(t) = R + v\,t$, with $t$ running continuously across the
whole frame, giving a time-varying round-trip delay $\tau(t) = 2 r(t) / c$.
\item The echo is formed as a copy of the transmitted signal evaluated at $t - \tau(t)$, scaled by
the target's amplitude. \textbf{No Doppler term is inserted by hand.} Because the delay changes as
the target moves, the carrier phase $2\pi f_c\,(t - \tau(t))$ advances slightly from one chirp to
the next, and that advance \emph{is} the Doppler shift.
\item The echoes of all targets are summed, with a delayed and attenuated echo added per target
where multipath is enabled.
\item Thermal noise of a calibrated variance is added, together with the clutter field if the
stratum requires one.
\item The sum is mixed with the transmitted signal, giving the beat signal.
\item The range transform is applied along fast time and the Doppler transform along slow time,
each with its window function.
\end{enumerate}

Only at that last step does the $\RadarRangeBins \times \RadarM$ range--Doppler map exist. The
consequence is that the shape of a target's response --- its mainlobe width, its sidelobes, the
scalloping loss it suffers when it falls between bin centres, and the leakage it produces along
each axis --- emerges from the signal processing rather than being modelled or drawn.

\subsection{Propagation, clutter and multipath}

\textbf{Propagation.} Received power follows the monostatic radar equation, falling as $1/R^4$.
Since the simulator synthesises signal amplitudes rather than powers, this is applied as an
amplitude scaling of $1/R^2$, which is the same law expressed in amplitude. In decibels both
readings coincide at a loss of $40\log_{10}(R/R_{\text{ref}})$, and this is how it is implemented,
referenced to $R_{\text{ref}} = \SI{25}{\metre}$ so that a target at that range keeps its nominal
amplitude. Propagation is enabled in every stratum, so the physics stays consistent across the
benchmark. The consequence is that range and difficulty are coupled: a distant target is genuinely
harder, as it is in practice.

\textbf{Clutter.} The zero-Doppler ridge is modelled as a band of elevated return around zero
velocity, with a Weibull texture rather than a Gaussian one. The shape parameter is swept, lower
values giving spikier clutter, so that the rate at which each detector degrades as clutter becomes
heavier-tailed is measurable rather than assumed. Its strength is specified as a clutter-to-noise
ratio, which keeps it meaningful when the noise level changes.

\textbf{Multipath.} A delayed, attenuated echo is optionally added, producing a secondary response
offset in range from the true target.

\section{Difficulty calibration}

\subsection{What signal-to-noise ratio means here}

Difficulty must be specified in a way that means the same thing to every detector, and the obvious
choice turns out to be the wrong one.

\textbf{The detector never sees the raw samples.} An SNR defined on the received time-domain
samples describes a signal that no detector in this report operates on. Between those samples and
the detector lie the two Fourier transforms, which concentrate a target's coherent energy into a
small region of the map while leaving noise spread across all of it. For this configuration that
concentration is worth \SI{\RadarProcGain}{\deci\bel}. A target specified at
\SI{-10}{\deci\bel} on the raw samples therefore does \emph{not} arrive \SI{10}{\deci\bel} below
the noise floor of the map; it arrives roughly \SI{30}{\deci\bel} \emph{above} it, and every
detector finds it trivially.

\textbf{Difficulty is therefore specified at the detector's input.} The quantity used throughout
this report is the \emph{cell SNR}: the ratio of a target's peak cell power to the background noise
power in one cell of the finished range--Doppler map,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SNR}_{\text{cell}} =
\frac{P_{\text{peak cell}}}{P_{\text{noise per cell}}} .
\label{eq:cellsnr}
\end{equation}
Given a requested cell SNR, the input noise variance needed to realise it is computed from the
window functions and the transform lengths.

\textbf{The achieved value is then measured, not assumed.} The realised cell SNR of a given target
generally differs a little from the requested one, for several reasons at once: propagation
attenuates it according to its range; it rarely falls exactly on a bin centre, so it loses some
peak power to scalloping; and the window functions redistribute energy between the peak cell and
its neighbours. Each target's achieved cell SNR is therefore measured on the generated frame and
stored beside its label, and it is that stored value --- never the requested one --- that every
analysis in this report uses.

\subsection{Where the difficulty sits}

Figure~\ref{fig:snrdist} shows the resulting distribution across the test split.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/Chapter3/ch3_fig5_snr_distribution.png}
\caption[Cell SNR distribution]{Measured cell SNR for every target in the test split. The spread
reflects range and target amplitude as well as the configured scene SNR, because propagation is
applied before the measurement.}
\label{fig:snrdist}
\end{figure}

The spread is deliberate, and the reasoning is simple. A useful benchmark needs many targets near
the region where detection changes from difficult to reliable. If almost every target were strong,
every detector would succeed and there would be little to compare. If almost every target were very
weak, every detector would fail and again there would be little to compare. The distribution was
therefore placed across the detection transition rather than above or below it.

An earlier configuration sat about \SI{2}{\deci\bel} lower. At that level roughly a fifth of all
targets fell below \SI{5}{\deci\bel} cell SNR, a region in which all the detectors evaluated here
had very low detection probability, and the clutter strata bunched near $\Pd \approx 0.15$, where
methods cannot be told apart. The scene level was raised by \SI{2}{\deci\bel} in response.

That adjustment belongs to dataset development and not to the reported comparison. It was made
while the benchmark was being designed, before the final splits were generated and well before the
test split was scored. It should be noted, though, that the observation prompting it came partly
from preliminary detector behaviour on earlier development data, so the calibration was not blind
to how detectors behave in general --- only to the results reported in
Chapters~\ref{chp:synthetic} and~\ref{chp:attribution}, which did not exist at the time.

\section{Dataset design}
\label{sec:datasets}

\subsection{Why stratified rather than randomly sampled}

When scenes were generated entirely at random, the difficult conditions occurred very rarely.
Target masking appeared in only \SI{1.1}{\percent} of frames and unresolved pairs in
\SI{0.4}{\percent}. An overall score computed on such a dataset is dominated by the much more
common easy cases, and changing the random composition would change the headline number even if the
detector had not changed at all.

The final benchmark instead generates each condition in equal quantity and reports results
condition by condition. This gives a reliable estimate within every scenario of interest, and it
makes the composition of the benchmark an explicit design choice rather than an accident of
sampling.

One point should be stated plainly to avoid a misreading. Equal strata are \emph{not} a claim about
how often these conditions occur in real driving; a road scene does not contain unresolved target
pairs a seventh of the time. This is a controlled benchmark, built so that each condition can be
measured reliably, and the per-condition results should be read that way rather than as a
prediction of average field performance.

Two design points in that table are worth drawing out.

The \texttt{clutter\_edge} stratum exists specifically because the literature review of
Section~\ref{sec:sota} identified it as the condition where a published learned detector loses. A
benchmark that omitted it would be systematically flattering.

Its velocity band needed care. The two clutter strata are meant to be different: in
\texttt{clutter} the target sits \emph{inside} the strong zero-Doppler ridge, whereas in
\texttt{clutter\_edge} the target should sit just \emph{outside} the ridge while the CFAR reference
window still overlaps it. The difficulty is that the ridge does not end where its nominal width
says it does --- the Doppler window spreads its energy outwards, so the region beside it is still
elevated (still \SI{13.6}{\deci\bel} above the noise floor at \SI{1.68}{\metre\per\second}). The
usable edge was therefore measured on generated frames rather than assumed. Had the nominal edge
been used, a substantial fraction of \texttt{clutter\_edge} targets would have landed inside the
residual clutter skirt and the stratum would simply have duplicated \texttt{clutter}.

The \texttt{unresolved} stratum places target pairs closer together than the measured resolution of
the range--Doppler representation used here, so the detectors evaluated in this report are not
expected to separate them reliably. It is retained as a sanity check: a method reporting high $\Pd$
on this stratum would point to a fault in the evaluation rather than to a capable detector, and
this is what exposed one such fault during development.

\section{Verification}

A simulator is an instrument, and an instrument that has not been checked cannot support a
measurement. Three checks were run before any detector result was produced.

\textbf{Point spread function.} An isolated target was placed and its response measured along both
axes, on-bin and deliberately off-bin. Figure~\ref{fig:psf} shows the result. The mainlobe spans
approximately two bins, which is what fixes the guard-cell count of the CFAR window in
Chapter~\ref{chp:detectors}: the guard region must cover a target's own spread, or a target would
inflate its own threshold.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/Chapter3/ch3_fig4_psf.png}
\caption[Measured point spread function]{Response of an isolated target along each axis, measured
rather than assumed. The off-bin case shows the loss incurred when a target does not fall at a bin
centre.}
\label{fig:psf}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Resolution limits.} Measured on the same data, two equal-strength targets require about
\SI{0.39}{\metre} of range separation to appear as separate peaks, rising to \SI{0.59}{\metre} when
one partner is weak, and \SI{1.16}{\metre\per\second} in Doppler. These measurements set the
separation ranges swept in the \texttt{masking} and \texttt{unresolved} strata, so that both sit
across the real limit rather than at an assumed one.

\textbf{Label alignment.} Every generated target was checked against the peak actually present in
the map. Alignment is exact above about \SI{22}{\deci\bel} cell SNR. Below that a small fraction of
labels do not coincide with the strongest local peak; this was investigated and traced to noise
spikes exceeding a weak target, which is a property of the physics rather than a labelling fault,
and it disappears at high SNR as a genuine bug would not.

\section{Conclusion}

This chapter specified the instrument that produces every synthetic result in this report.

Simulation was chosen because it supplies exact ground truth, independent control over each
difficulty, and enough examples of the rare conditions that actually separate detectors. Its cost
is external validity, which is why Chapter~\ref{chp:realdata} exists.

The sensor reproduces the range--Doppler grid geometry and bin spacing of the real dataset used
later, so that a physical separation corresponds to the same number of cells in both domains. The
effective bandwidth of \SI{\RadarB}{\mega\hertz} was derived from that dataset's own published grid
rather than from its quoted bandwidth figure, which is inconsistent with it. Matching the grid
removes a scaling confound; it does not make target responses identical, and
Chapter~\ref{chp:realdata} treats that remaining difference explicitly.

Targets are not drawn onto the map. They are specified physically and passed through the full
signal chain, so the shape of a response, its sidelobes and its scalloping loss emerge from the
processing rather than from a model of it. Difficulty is measured at the detector as cell SNR and
recorded per target, so the value used in analysis is the one realised rather than the one
requested.

The benchmark is divided into eight equal conditions spanning both the regime where CA-CFAR is
close to optimal and the regimes where it is documented to fail --- including one chosen
specifically because a published learned detector loses there. Five independent splits keep network
training, threshold selection, CFAR window selection, the SNR sweep and the single scored test set
apart, so no detector is tuned on the data used to score it. The simulator's point spread function,
resolution limits and label alignment were all verified before any detector was run.

Chapter~\ref{chp:detectors} now specifies the two detectors that operate on these frames, and fixes
the evaluation protocol before any comparative result is produced.

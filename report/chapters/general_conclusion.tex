\chapter*{General Conclusion}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{General Conclusion}
\markboth{General Conclusion}{General Conclusion}

This project asked when a learned local radar detector actually provides an advantage over classical
CFAR, and what is required for that advantage to survive the move from controlled simulation to real
measurements. It answered that question in five stages, each of which changed the answer the
previous one would have given.

To do so it built: a simulator producing labelled FMCW range--Doppler maps with a calibrated
cell-SNR definition; four CFAR detectors on a measured window geometry; a
\TinyParams{}-parameter convolutional detector whose receptive field equals the CFAR window by
construction; and one shared evaluation path --- the same candidate extraction, the same one-to-one
matching, the same statistics. \emph{Within each experiment, all detector arms share the same
evaluation rule and operating-point definition.} The synthetic and real-data experiments necessarily
use different operating-point protocols, because real data supplies no false-alarm axis to match on,
and they are therefore compared only within domain.

\section*{Answering the research questions}

\textbf{RQ1 --- what a learned local rule adds, in-domain.} On a fresh held-out synthetic test set,
at matched \emph{measured} false-alarm rate, the learned detector establishes gains in five challenge
conditions, ties in homogeneous clutter, and shows no significant difference on the
resolution-limited stress case after correction. It reaches $\Pd = 0.5$ at a cell SNR
\SI{\SynSnrGainDL}{\deci\bel} below CA-CFAR. This establishes potential under controlled conditions;
it establishes nothing about deployment.

\textbf{RQ2 --- where the advantage comes from.} Attribution by intervention rules out the cheap
explanation: giving CFAR the same integrated mainlobe energy makes it worse, not better, on every
condition. Guard-region access contributes a small tolerant-detection benefit and simultaneously
broadens the response, hurting exact centring. Most usefully, the frozen arm's localisation deficit
is largely attributable to its flat $3\times3$ supervision rather than to learned detection as such
--- concentrating the label removes most of it at negligible detection cost. Together these narrow
the field to an adaptive learned decision rule without uniquely identifying its mechanism, which the
experiments were not designed to do.

\textbf{RQ3 --- whether it survives transfer.} It does not. Evaluated zero-shot on the CARRADA test
split, with no retraining, fine-tuning or real-data recalibration, the synthetic-trained network and
CA-CFAR are \textbf{statistically indistinguishable} in exact dense-mask recall (\RoneTinyCNN{}
against \RoneCFARCA{}; sequence-balanced difference \RoneDelta{}, 95\,\% CI
[\RoneCIlo{}, \RoneCIhi{}]), while the network emits \RoneBurdenRatio{} times more unmatched
detections per frame. The classical detector does not reverse the result; the learned advantage
simply disappears, and the learned arm pays for parity with output density.

\textbf{RQ4 --- whether the training domain was the limiting factor.} Substantially, yes. Rebuilding
the synthetic training background from CARRADA \emph{training}-split statistics alone --- with no
real frame entering neural optimisation or threshold calibration --- raised exact test recall from
\CiTestOrig{} to \CiTestNew{} (\CiTestDelta{}, 95\,\% CI [\CiTestCIlo{}, \CiTestCIhi{}]), positive on
every test sequence, and closed the near-range deficit. But it did so at \CiTestBurdenRatio{} times
the output density, and burden-matched analysis showed CA-CFAR still ahead once output was equalised.
Domain alignment recovered recall; it did not by itself establish superiority.

\textbf{RQ5 --- what real supervision adds.} Fine-tuned on CARRADA training sequences, with the
operating point fixed on held-out training sequences under a burden constraint before the evaluation
split was opened, the same network reached exact recall \FtValFt{} against CA-CFAR's \FtValCFAR{} on
previously unscored validation sequences --- a difference of \FtDelta{} (95\,\% CI
[\FtCIlo{}, \FtCIhi{}]) at a slightly \emph{lower} unmatched-detection burden (\FtValBurdenFt{}
against \FtValBurdenCFAR{}). Against the domain-informed synthetic arm, fine-tuning established
\emph{no} recall gain (\FtVsCiDelta{}, CI [\FtVsCiCIlo{}, \FtVsCiCIhi{}]); what it changed was the
recall--output trade-off. At CA-CFAR's own burden of \FtMatchedBurden{} detections per frame the
fine-tuned arm reads \FtMatchedFt{} against the synthetic-only arm's \FtMatchedCi{} and CA-CFAR's
\FtMatchedCFAR{}.

\section*{What this means for CFAR versus deep learning}

\textbf{This study does not support replacing CFAR universally with deep learning.} CA-CFAR provided
a strong training-free baseline and transferred to a new real domain without adaptation of any kind.
TinyCNN demonstrated a higher observed performance ceiling, but that ceiling depended on the training
distribution. Controlled synthetic gains were lost under zero-shot real transfer, substantially
recovered through target-domain-informed simulation, and converted into a recall--efficiency
advantage only after supervised real-domain adaptation. \textbf{The practical choice is therefore
conditional: when representative training data are unavailable, CFAR remains a robust default; when
the deployment domain can be represented during training, a learned local detector can exceed it.}

Two qualifications belong with that statement.

\textbf{Recall alone would have been misleading at every stage.} Read on recall only, the five stages
say: the learned detector wins, then ties, then wins large, then wins. Read with output burden beside
it, they say something more useful --- that the learned detector's standing depends on how much output
it is allowed to emit, and that the one stage where it leads on both axes at once is the stage where
it received real supervision.

\textbf{The final comparison gives the learned detector something CA-CFAR does not get.} The
fine-tuned network is supervised with the same dense segmentation support the primary metric scores
against. That is legitimate supervised adaptation --- it is what the experiment set out to test --- but
it aligns the network's output geometry with the truth geometry, and the classical detector has no
equivalent opportunity. The defensible statement is that \emph{under supervised target-domain
adaptation, the learned detector outperformed CA-CFAR on this evaluation}, not that it is inherently
the better detector. The interval also only narrowly excludes zero: its lower bound is \FtCIlo{} on
\FtNseq{} sequences, with the fine-tuned arm ahead on \FtSeqAhead{} of \FtNseq{}, so substantial
sequence-level uncertainty remains.

\section*{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{One simulator, one real dataset, one architecture.} Nothing here supports a claim about
learned detectors in general.
\item \textbf{No false-alarm measurement on real data.} CARRADA annotates the objects that were
labelled, not every scatterer present, so the learned arm's denser output is an output-burden measure
and can never be priced against recall as a false-alarm rate.
\item \textbf{The domain intervention changed several properties jointly.} It supports a causal
statement about the combined intervention and about nothing narrower; no individual background
property has been isolated. It is also a \emph{partial} domain-gap reduction: the Doppler correlation
structure was deliberately left unmatched, because correcting it would have required changing the
range--Doppler processing window that also defines the target point spread function and the guard
geometry.
\item \textbf{The attribution experiments run on validation data.} They explain the frozen benchmark;
they do not extend it, and no arm they identify as better was itself frozen and scored on held-out
data.
\item \textbf{The fine-tune was deliberately small and had not converged.} Every seed reached the
ten-epoch ceiling with its development loss still falling, so the final result is a lower bound on
that configuration rather than its best achievable behaviour.
\item \textbf{The post-processing protocol was revised during the work}, on synthetic development
data, and the real data re-scored under it. Appendix~\ref{app:prereg} records what changed.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Future work}

The results point to specific next experiments rather than general ambitions.

\begin{itemize}
\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Separate what the domain intervention bundled}, with one ablation per property ---
multi-look-like smoothness, ridge modelling, range non-stationarity, correlation structure --- to
convert a combined causal statement into individual ones.
\item \textbf{Measure how much real data is actually needed.} The fine-tuning result used all
fifteen fitting sequences; a sample-efficiency curve over increasing fractions of real labelled data
would answer the question a practitioner actually faces.
\item \textbf{Prefer domain randomisation to calibration against one dataset.} Randomising background
statistics over a plausible range would test whether the recovered transfer generalises or is
specific to CARRADA, and a second real dataset would test the same thing directly.
\item \textbf{Make output burden part of the objective.} Burden mattered as much as recall at every
stage, yet it was only ever measured, never optimised. A burden-aware loss or calibration criterion
follows naturally.
\item \textbf{Adapt with weaker supervision}, since dense mask supervision is expensive and it is
open whether weak, self-supervised or unsupervised adaptation recovers any of the same benefit.
\item \textbf{Carry the improved supervision design through a complete benchmark.} The label
ablation showed concentrated supervision substantially improves localisation at negligible detection
cost, but that arm was never frozen, never scored on held-out data and never taken to real
measurements.
\end{itemize}

Larger architectures are deliberately \emph{not} the first recommendation. The behaviour that
dominated every stage of this project --- domain sensitivity, operating-point dependence, the
recall-versus-burden trade --- was visible at \TinyParams{} parameters, and is better understood there
than hidden inside a larger model.

\section*{Final statement}

CFAR is the stronger training-free detector: cheap, interpretable, and transferable to a real sensor
it had never seen with no change at all. A small learned detector demonstrated the higher observed
performance ceiling in this study, but reached it only when the deployment domain was represented
during training. The practical question is therefore not whether deep learning beats CFAR, but
whether enough domain knowledge and representative training data are available to justify that
ceiling. Where they are, it is worth the effort. Where they are not, CFAR is still the right answer.

\section*{What this project set out to do, and what it built}

The question was what a small learned local decision rule adds to radar target detection when it is
compared against CFAR on genuinely equal terms. Answering it required a comparison that could be
trusted: comparable local context, a single evaluator, operating points matched at
\emph{measured} rather than design false-alarm rates, a per-condition best-CFAR comparator, eight
seeds rather than one, and results reported across a tolerance ladder. That protocol, together with
a simulator whose bin geometry matches the real dataset used later, is the project's main
methodological contribution.

\section*{What the learned detector adds, synthetically}

On a held-out split read once, the network establishes a gain on the five resolvable challenge
conditions after correction for multiple comparisons. Homogeneous clutter is a practical tie, and
the unresolved stratum --- a resolution and protocol stress test rather than an ordinary competitive
condition --- shows a small difference that does not survive correction. The margin is largest where
CFAR is documented to fail, reaching \SynClutterEdgeDiff{} at clutter edges, and in decibels the
network reaches half detection at a cell SNR \SI{\SynSnrGainDL}{\deci\bel} below CA-CFAR. This is a
real advantage of ordinary size for this literature, not an outlier.

\section*{What the attribution experiments taught us}

Three pre-registered interventions narrowed the mechanism without isolating a single cause.

Energy integration is ruled out: given CFAR the same integrated mainlobe energy, it performs worse
rather than better. Guard-region access contributes to detection, but modestly, and it broadens the
network's response.

The most useful finding concerns supervision. The frozen benchmark arm places its detections less
precisely than CFAR, and that behaviour is largely \emph{induced by the flat $3\times3$ label rather
than inherent to the learned detector}. Concentrating the supervision reduces Doppler error from
\EthreeFlatDv{} bins to \EthreeGaussDv{} under a PSF-weighted label and \EthreePointDv{} under a
point label, while tolerant detection performance is essentially unchanged
(\EthreeGaussDeltaPd{} for the weighted label). A limitation of the benchmark configuration is
therefore a design choice with a measured, better alternative --- which is a more useful result than
the deficit itself.

\section*{What survived transfer to real data}

Evaluated zero-shot on CARRADA --- no retraining, no fine-tuning, and no real data used for
threshold or post-processing choices --- the two detectors are statistically indistinguishable at
exact annotated support: a sequence-balanced difference of \RoneDelta{} whose
\SI{95}{\percent} interval $[\RoneCIlo, \RoneCIhi]$ includes zero. The network leads at wider
tolerance gates and trails in the nearest range block, and it reaches comparable recall while
emitting \RoneBurdenRatio{} times as many unmatched detections per frame.

The synthetic advantage therefore does not transfer cleanly. It is not replaced by a symmetrical
advantage for the classical detector either: on real data the two are hard to separate on recall,
and easy to separate on how much output they produce to achieve it. That leads to the project's most
transferable conclusion. \textbf{Detection probability alone is not sufficient to rank these
detectors.} Candidate density, localisation and post-processing all shape deployment behaviour, and
the real-data comparison proved sensitive to a post-processing constant that the synthetic benchmark
is almost insensitive to.

\section*{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{One simulator, one real dataset, one architecture.} Nothing here supports a claim
about learned detectors in general.
\item \textbf{The real-data comparison is one-sided.} CARRADA supports no false-alarm measurement,
so the network's larger output cannot be priced against its recall.
\item \textbf{No causal intervention across domains.} The domain audit locates differences; it does
not establish that any of them caused the loss of the margin.
\item \textbf{The attribution experiments run on validation data only.} They explain the frozen
benchmark; they do not extend it, and no arm they identify as better was ever itself frozen and
scored on held-out data.
\item \textbf{The post-processing protocol was revised during the work}, on synthetic development
data, and the real data re-scored under it. Appendix~\ref{app:prereg} records what changed.
\end{itemize}

\section*{The most useful next experiment}

\textbf{Carry the improved supervision design through a complete frozen benchmark and external
validation.} The label ablation established on development data that concentrated supervision
substantially improves localisation at negligible detection cost, but that arm was never frozen,
never scored on a held-out split, and never taken to real data. Doing so would test whether removing
the localisation deficit also changes the transfer result --- in particular whether a sharper
response reduces the unmatched-detection burden that the real-data comparison turns on. That is a
single, well-defined experiment which the present evidence makes worth running.

Three smaller directions follow: evaluating on a second real dataset, since one measurement is not a
norm; training on deliberately modified background statistics, to convert the domain audit's
correlations into a causal statement; and treating candidate extraction as part of the detector
rather than as a fixed constant, since it was found to matter on extended real objects and not on
synthetic point targets.

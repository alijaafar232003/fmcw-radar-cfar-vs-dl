\section{Motivation}

Automotive radar occupies a position in the perception stack that no other sensor holds. A camera
fails in darkness, fog and direct glare; a lidar degrades in rain, snow and airborne dust. A
millimetre-wave radar continues to measure range and radial velocity through all of them, and it
measures velocity \emph{directly}, from the Doppler shift, rather than inferring it by differencing
successive frames. For an advanced driver assistance system that must decide whether the object
ahead is closing at \SI{2}{\metre\per\second} or \SI{20}{\metre\per\second}, that distinction is not
a convenience but the basis of the decision.

The frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW) waveform is what makes this affordable. A linear
frequency ramp, mixed with its own echo, produces a low-frequency beat whose frequency encodes range
and whose phase evolution across successive ramps encodes velocity. Two Fourier transforms convert a
block of received samples into a \emph{range--Doppler map}: a two-dimensional image in which a target
appears as a localised concentration of energy at its range and its radial velocity. Everything that
follows in a radar processing chain --- clustering, tracking, classification, and ultimately the
decision to brake --- operates on what is extracted from that image.

Extraction is a detection problem, and it is the first irreversible step in the chain. A target that
the detector misses is not recovered by any downstream stage: the tracker never receives it, the
classifier never sees it, the planner never accounts for it. A spurious detection propagates in the
opposite direction, consuming track resources and, in the worst case, triggering an intervention
that was never warranted. The detector therefore sets a ceiling on the performance of everything
built above it.

For roughly fifty years the standard answer has been the constant false alarm rate (CFAR) family of
detectors. Their central idea is elegant and remains compelling: rather than compare each cell
against a fixed threshold --- which would require knowing the noise power, a quantity that varies
with temperature, terrain, weather and interference --- estimate the local background from the cells
surrounding the cell under test, and scale that estimate by a multiplier chosen to hold the
false-alarm rate constant. The detector adapts to conditions it was never told about. It is
analytically tractable, requires no training data, costs almost nothing to compute, and its
behaviour can be predicted in closed form under stated assumptions.

Those assumptions are also its limitation, and they were characterised early. Gandhi and
Kassam~\cite{gandhi1988cfar} showed that cell-averaging CFAR degrades sharply in exactly the
situations that matter operationally: at clutter edges, where the reference window straddles two
different background levels and the threshold is set from the wrong one; and under target masking,
where a second target inside the reference window inflates the background estimate and suppresses
the detection of the first. Variants --- greatest-of, smallest-of, order-statistic --- each repair
one failure mode and worsen another. Their conclusion was that no single CFAR detector performs well
across all backgrounds, and that an adaptive approach may be required if operating conditions change.

That conclusion is, in effect, an invitation to learn the detector from data, and over the past
decade a substantial body of work has accepted it. Convolutional networks applied to range--Doppler
maps have been reported to outperform CFAR by margins ranging from a fraction of a decibel to
several orders of magnitude in false-alarm rate. The claims are, on their face, considerable.

\section{Problem statement}

The difficulty is that these claims are hard to compare, and in several cases hard to interpret at
all.

A comparison between a classical and a learned detector is only meaningful if both are given the
same information, evaluated at the same operating point, and scored by the same rule. In much of the
published literature at least one of these conditions fails, usually silently. A network may be
given a preprocessed input --- windowed, matched-filtered, or presented at several resolutions ---
while the CFAR baseline receives the raw magnitude. A network may observe a spatial context far
larger than the CFAR reference window, so that the two are not solving the same local problem. A
network may be retrained for each test condition while the classical detector keeps a single global
parameter set. False-alarm rates may be matched by \emph{design}, using the analytic CFAR
expression, rather than by measurement --- and it is well documented, including by
authors reporting learned wins~\cite{yavuz2021radcnn}, that CFAR detectors do not attain their
design $\Pfa$ in clutter. Finally, a detection may be scored as correct if it lands within some
tolerance of the truth, in which case a detector that responds over a broad region is rewarded for
imprecision.

Each of these is defensible in isolation. Together they make the reported margin a property of the
experimental setup as much as of the detector. A reader is left unable to answer three questions
that ought to be separable:

\begin{enumerate}
\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Is there a real advantage} when the two detectors are given genuinely equivalent
information and scored at a genuinely matched operating point?
\item \textbf{Where does it come from?} A margin that is not attributed to a mechanism is not
understood, and cannot be relied upon to appear again under different conditions.
\item \textbf{Does it survive outside the training domain?} A detector trained on simulated data and
evaluated on the same simulator has demonstrated that it learned the simulator. Whether it learned
\emph{radar} is a separate question, and one that the majority of the literature does not ask.
\end{enumerate}

The third question carries particular weight for a learned detector, and for a reason specific to
this problem. CFAR estimates its threshold from the data in front of it at inference time, so its
adaptation mechanism travels with it to any domain. A trained network carries its adaptation in
frozen weights, fitted to whatever distribution produced its training data. If that distribution and
the deployment distribution differ, there is no mechanism by which the network can notice.

\section{Objectives}

This project sets out to answer those three questions under conditions strict enough that the
answers mean something. Four objectives follow.

\begin{description}
\itemsep3pt
\item[O1 --- Build a controlled comparison.] Construct a classical arm and a learned arm that
receive the same input representation, observe the same spatial context, and are scored by a single
evaluator at a matched \emph{measured} false-alarm rate. The learned arm's receptive field is
matched deliberately to the CFAR reference window, so that neither detector sees information the
other does not.

\item[O2 --- Measure the margin.] Quantify the difference across a suite of conditions chosen to
include both the regimes where CFAR is known to be near-optimal and those where it is known to fail,
with confidence intervals and correction for multiple comparisons.

\item[O3 --- Attribute it.] Determine \emph{why} any margin exists, by separating rival explanations
experimentally rather than by argument. Two candidates are identified in the literature and both
predict a learned advantage in homogeneous conditions, so neither can be accepted without excluding
the other.

\item[O4 --- Test external validity.] Evaluate the frozen detectors, without retraining or
recalibration of any kind, on real automotive radar measurements from a public dataset, under a
protocol registered and hash-frozen before the test data were scored.
\end{description}

An objective that is deliberately \emph{not} adopted should be stated alongside them. This project
does not aim to produce a state-of-the-art detector. A larger network, trained on real data with
modern augmentation, would almost certainly detect more targets than the one built here. The aim is
to measure a difference under conditions where the measurement can be trusted, and a small
well-understood result serves that aim better than a large unexplained one.

\section{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\itemsep4pt
\item \textbf{A controlled like-for-like protocol.} The learned detector's receptive field is matched
to the CFAR window, both arms are scored by one evaluator through a single code path, operating
points are matched at measured rather than design $\Pfa$, and results are reported across a
tolerance ladder rather than at one gate. Each of these choices closes a specific asymmetry
identified in the literature, and each is documented with the asymmetry it addresses.

\item \textbf{A quantified and externally calibrated margin.} On synthetic data the learned detector
leads by \SynSnrGainDL{}\,dB of cell signal-to-noise ratio at matched measured $\Pfa$, reaching
\SynClutterEdgeDiff{} in $\Pd$ at clutter edges --- a figure that falls inside the band independently
reported for clutter transitions on real X-band measurements~\cite{gusland2020deeptemporal}. The
CFAR loss hypothesised as the cause of learned wins in homogeneous
conditions~\cite{lallouet2023loss} is measured directly rather than assumed, at
\SynCfarLossCA{}\,dB against a clairvoyant bound.

\item \textbf{An attribution programme} that decomposes the margin into named mechanisms, each with
an experiment capable of falsifying it, rather than reporting the margin as a single unexplained
number.

\item \textbf{A pre-registered zero-shot external validation.} The frozen detectors are evaluated on
the CARRADA automotive radar dataset~\cite{ouaknine2021carrada} with no real-data training,
fine-tuning or recalibration, under a protocol frozen by cryptographic hash before any test frame
was scored. No published work in the comparable family performs this evaluation. \textbf{The
ordering established on synthetic data reverses}, and the report treats that reversal as the
principal finding rather than as an anomaly to be explained away.

\item \textbf{A reproducibility apparatus} proportionate to the claims: frozen model and detector
artefacts, isolation manifests protecting completed experiments from later modification, immutable
raw evidence retained alongside the derived tables, and a corrections log recording every analysis
error found and superseded during the work.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Report outline}

The report follows the order in which the work was done, because several of its conclusions depend
on what was known at the time a decision was taken.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chp:background}} develops the radar background needed for the rest: the FMCW
waveform, range and Doppler estimation, the construction of the range--Doppler map, and the CFAR
family with its documented failure modes. It then reviews learned detection, and argues that the
published literature separates into families whose metrics are not interchangeable --- a distinction
the remainder of the report observes strictly.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chp:simulator}} describes the simulator, the radar specification, and the design
of the labelled datasets. The radar parameters were chosen to reproduce the range--Doppler bin
geometry of the real dataset used in Chapter~\ref{chp:realdata}, so that a target occupies a
comparable number of cells in both domains and any later transfer failure cannot be dismissed as a
scaling artefact.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chp:detectors}} specifies both detectors and freezes the evaluation protocol:
peak extraction, matching, the operating-point definition, and the statistical treatment. The
protocol is fixed before any comparative result is produced.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chp:synthetic}} reports the synthetic benchmark across the seven conditions,
including the detection probability as a function of cell signal-to-noise ratio that puts the result
into the units the literature uses.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chp:attribution}} asks where the measured margin comes from, and reports the
experiments that separate the competing explanations.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{chp:realdata}} evaluates the frozen detectors zero-shot on real automotive radar
data under the pre-registered protocol, audits the domain difference before scoring, reports the
reversal, and places both halves of the project against the published literature.

The \textbf{General Conclusion} states what the evidence supports, what it does not, and what the
natural continuations are. Three appendices record the corrections log, the frozen pre-registration
and the supporting tables.

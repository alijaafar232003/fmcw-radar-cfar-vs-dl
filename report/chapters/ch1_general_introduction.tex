\section{Motivation}

Automotive radar has a distinctive role in the sensor suite of a modern vehicle. Cameras can degrade
under poor illumination, glare and reduced visibility. Lidar can be affected by rain, snow and
airborne particles. Millimetre-wave radar is comparatively robust under many of these conditions.
It also measures radial velocity directly, from the Doppler shift, rather than estimating it by
comparing successive frames. For a driver assistance system that must distinguish an object closing
at \SI{2}{\metre\per\second} from one closing at \SI{20}{\metre\per\second}, a direct velocity
measurement is valuable.

The frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW) waveform makes this practical at low cost. The radar
transmits a linear frequency ramp, called a chirp, and mixes the received echo with the transmitted
signal. The result is a low-frequency beat tone. Its frequency gives the target range, and its phase
change from one chirp to the next gives the radial velocity. Two Fourier transforms turn a block of
received samples into a \emph{range--Doppler} (RD) map: a two-dimensional image in which a target
appears as a concentration of energy at its range and its radial velocity. Everything downstream ---
clustering, tracking, classification, and ultimately the driving decision --- works from what is
extracted from this map.

Extracting targets from the RD map is a detection problem, and it is the first stage of that chain.
A tracker can bridge an occasional missed detection, but a target that is missed persistently cannot
be tracked, classified or acted upon, because no measurement of it reaches those stages. Excessive
false detections cause the opposite problem: they consume tracking resources and can trigger
unnecessary responses. Detection therefore sets a limit on how well the later stages can perform.

For about fifty years the standard solution has been the constant false alarm rate (CFAR) family of
detectors. Comparing each cell against a fixed threshold would require knowing the noise power,
which changes with temperature, terrain, weather and interference. Instead, CFAR estimates the local
background from cells surrounding the cell under test (CUT), and scales that estimate by a
multiplier chosen to hold the false-alarm rate constant. The detector therefore adapts to conditions
it was never told about. CFAR needs no training data, is cheap to compute, and its behaviour can be
predicted mathematically, provided the background behaves as assumed.

Those assumptions are also its weakness, and this was documented early. Gandhi and
Kassam~\cite{gandhi1988cfar} showed that cell-averaging CFAR degrades in situations that matter in
practice. At a clutter edge, the reference window covers two different background levels and the
threshold is set from the wrong one. Under target masking, a second target inside the reference
window raises the background estimate and suppresses detection of the first. The main variants ---
greatest-of, smallest-of and order-statistic --- each improve one failure mode and worsen another.
Their conclusion was that no single CFAR detector performs well across all backgrounds, and that an
adaptive approach may be needed when operating conditions change.

This naturally suggests learning the detector from data instead of designing it by hand, and much
recent work has done exactly that. Convolutional networks applied to RD maps have been reported to
outperform CFAR, with improvements ranging from a fraction of a decibel to large reductions in
false-alarm rate.

\section{Problem statement}
\label{sec:problem}

These reported improvements are difficult to compare with one another, and difficult to interpret
individually.

A comparison between a classical and a learned detector is informative only if both receive
comparable information, are evaluated at the same operating point, and are scored by the same rule.
Across the reviewed literature, these conditions are not always simultaneously satisfied. Five
differences recur:

\begin{itemize}
\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Input preprocessing.} The network may receive a windowed, matched-filtered or
multi-resolution input, while the CFAR baseline receives raw magnitude data.
\item \textbf{Spatial context.} The network may observe a much larger region than the CFAR reference
window, so the two are not solving the same local problem.
\item \textbf{Retraining.} The network may be retrained for each test condition, while the classical
detector keeps a single global parameter set.
\item \textbf{Operating point.} False-alarm rates are often matched by \emph{design}, using the
analytic CFAR expression, rather than by measurement. CFAR detectors do not reach their design
$\Pfa$ in clutter, a point reported by authors who also report learned
wins~\cite{yavuz2021radcnn}.
\item \textbf{Scoring tolerance.} A detection may count as correct if it falls within some distance
of the true position. A detector whose response is spread over a broad region then benefits from
that tolerance.
\end{itemize}

Each of these choices can be reasonable on its own. Together, however, they mean that the reported
improvement depends partly on how the experiment was set up, and not only on the detector itself.

A second difficulty compounds the first. A detector trained on simulated data and evaluated on the
same simulator has shown that it learned the simulator; whether it learned \emph{radar} is a
separate question, and one that much of the literature does not test. That question matters
particularly here, for a reason specific to the problem. CFAR estimates its threshold from the data
in front of it at inference time, so it keeps adapting in whatever domain it is used. A
conventionally frozen feed-forward network has no explicit mechanism to re-estimate its decision
rule when the deployment distribution changes: its behaviour is fixed by weights fitted to the
training distribution.

This shapes the central question of the project, and it is deliberately not ``is a CNN better than
CFAR?''. A single ranking would hide the thing that turns out to matter most. The question this
thesis answers is:

\begin{quote}
\textbf{When does a learned local radar detector actually provide an advantage over classical CFAR,
and what is required for that advantage to survive transfer from controlled simulation to real radar
data?}
\end{quote}

\section{Research questions}
\label{sec:rqs}

Five questions follow, each answered by one stage of the work.

\begin{description}
\itemsep4pt
\item[RQ1 --- The controlled comparison.] Under controlled synthetic conditions, with comparable
local range--Doppler context and a matched measured operating point, what detection advantage does a
small learned local decision rule provide over classical CFAR?
\item[RQ2 --- The mechanism.] What detector characteristics explain the learned gains and their
trade-offs? Three are tested: energy integration over the mainlobe, the role of supervision in
localisation, and access to the guard region.
\item[RQ3 --- Transfer.] Does the learned advantage survive zero-shot transfer from simulated FMCW
data to real range--Doppler measurements?
\item[RQ4 --- The training domain.] If it does not, does reducing the \emph{measured}
synthetic-to-real background mismatch improve transfer, without any real radar frame entering neural
optimisation?
\item[RQ5 --- Real adaptation.] With real-domain supervision permitted, can the learned detector
beat CA-CFAR at a comparable output burden, on real sequences it has never been scored on?
\end{description}

RQ1 and RQ2 concern the detector. RQ3, RQ4 and RQ5 concern the distribution it was trained on, and
they are the reason this thesis does not end with its synthetic benchmark.

\section{Objectives}

Six objectives operationalise the five research questions above.

\begin{description}
\itemsep3pt
\item[O1 --- Build a controlled comparison.] Construct a classical arm and a learned arm that work
from the same range--Doppler data, observe comparable local context, and are scored by one evaluator
at a matched \emph{measured} false-alarm rate. Chapter~\ref{chp:detectors} states what the two arms
share and what they do not.

\item[O2 --- Measure the margin.] Quantify the difference across conditions chosen to include both
regimes where CFAR is close to optimal and regimes where it is known to fail, with confidence
intervals and a correction for multiple comparisons.

\item[O3 --- Attribute the margin.] Pair each competing explanation with an experiment
capable of disproving it, rather than arguing for one of them. Such experiments can support or rule
out particular mechanisms; they are not expected to identify a single cause.

\item[O4 --- Test external validity.] Evaluate both frozen detectors, with no retraining or
recalibration of any kind, on real automotive radar data from a public dataset, under a protocol
fixed before the evaluation data were scored.

\item[O5 --- Test the training domain.] If transfer fails, measure the difference between the
simulated and real domains, rebuild the synthetic training background from target-domain statistics
alone, and re-measure transfer --- keeping every real radar frame out of neural optimisation. This
separates ``the detector cannot transfer'' from ``it was trained on the wrong distribution''.

\item[O6 --- Test what real supervision adds.] Permit real-domain adaptation and ask whether the
same small network can exceed the classical baseline on previously unscored real sequences at a
comparable output burden, with the operating point fixed on training data before the evaluation
split is opened.
\end{description}

One objective is deliberately excluded. This project does not aim to produce a state-of-the-art
detector. A larger network, trained on real data with modern augmentation, would very likely detect
more targets than the one built here. The aim is to measure a difference under conditions where the
measurement can be trusted, and for that purpose a small, well-understood result is more useful than
a large unexplained one.

\section{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\itemsep5pt
\item \textbf{A controlled like-for-like comparison of CFAR and a learned local detector.} The
learned detector's receptive field is matched to the spatial footprint of the CFAR window; both arms
are scored by one evaluator through a single code path; operating points are matched at measured
rather than design $\Pfa$; and results are reported across a tolerance ladder rather than at a
single gate. Each choice removes one of the asymmetries listed in Section~\ref{sec:problem}. The
resulting margin is reported per condition with confidence intervals and a correction for multiple
comparisons, and expressed in decibels of cell SNR so that it can be placed beside the comparable
literature.

\item \textbf{Attribution by intervention.} Three experiments each pair a candidate explanation with
a measurement capable of disproving it. One outcome is directly useful: the frozen benchmark's
localisation deficit is largely attributable to its supervision, and substantially removable, rather
than an inherent property of the learned detector.

\item \textbf{A zero-shot synthetic-to-real evaluation.} The frozen detectors are evaluated on the
CARRADA automotive radar dataset~\cite{ouaknine2021carrada} with no real-data training, fine-tuning
or recalibration. Ground truth, matching, metrics and the statistical plan were pre-specified and
frozen before any evaluation frame was scored. No directly comparable zero-shot experiment was
identified in the reviewed corpus.

\item \textbf{A target-domain-informed synthetic intervention.} The simulated background was rebuilt
from statistics measured on the CARRADA \emph{training} split alone --- smoothness, range dependence
and zero-Doppler ridge structure --- with no real frame entering neural optimisation or threshold
calibration. Exact real-data recall rose from \CiTestOrig{} to \CiTestNew{}, showing that a
substantial part of the transfer failure was a property of the training distribution rather than of
the detector. The same experiment shows the limit of that reading: the recovery came with
\CiTestBurdenRatio{} times the output density, and did not survive burden matching against CA-CFAR.

\item \textbf{A real-domain adaptation result with burden-aware evaluation.} Fine-tuned on CARRADA
training sequences with its operating point fixed on training data alone, the same
\TinyParams{}-parameter network reached exact recall \FtValFt{} against CA-CFAR's \FtValCFAR{} on
previously unscored validation sequences, at a slightly \emph{lower} unmatched-detection burden.
Within the reviewed corpus, no directly comparable experiment was identified that carries one small
detector through controlled synthetic comparison, zero-shot transfer, domain-informed retraining and
real adaptation under a single evaluation protocol.

\item \textbf{An empirical argument against single-number detector comparisons.} Across the five
stages, detection probability alone would have supported three different and partly contradictory
conclusions. Output burden, training domain, tolerance and operating point each changed the answer,
and the strongest learned result in the thesis is one where recall and burden had to be read
together. A related protocol-sensitivity finding is reported separately: on annotated objects larger
than the suppression window, the rule that converts a dense score map into candidates moved the
headline result, where on synthetic point targets it did not.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Report outline}

The report follows the order in which the work was carried out, because several conclusions depend
on what was known when a given decision was made. Figure~\ref{fig:roadmap} shows that order as a
single roadmap: five stages, one question each, and the conclusion they lead to.

this figure is for orientation, and the chapters carry the evidence.}
\label{fig:roadmap}

\begin{description}
\itemsep3pt
\item[Chapter~\ref{chp:background}, \emph{Radar Background and State of the Art}.] The FMCW
waveform, the RD map, the CFAR family and its documented failure modes, learned detection, and the
gap in the published comparisons that this work addresses.
\item[Chapter~\ref{chp:simulator}, \emph{Simulator, Radar Specification and Dataset Design}.] The
simulator, the radar parameters --- chosen to reproduce the real dataset's RD bin geometry --- and
the labelled datasets.
\item[Chapter~\ref{chp:detectors}, \emph{Detectors and Evaluation Protocol}.] Both detectors and the
shared evaluation protocol: candidate extraction, matching, operating point and statistics.
\item[Chapter~\ref{chp:synthetic}, \emph{Synthetic Benchmark Results}.] Stage~1: the controlled
comparison across the seven scored conditions, including detection probability against cell SNR.
\item[Chapter~\ref{chp:attribution}, \emph{Attribution Experiments}.] Stage~2: three interventions
that separate the competing explanations for the measured margin.
\item[Chapter~\ref{chp:realdata}, \emph{Real-Data External Validation}.] Stage~3: the frozen
detectors evaluated zero-shot on real automotive radar data, and a domain audit explaining what
changed.
\item[Chapter~\ref{chp:domaingap}, \emph{Domain Alignment and Real-Data Adaptation}.] Stages~4
and~5: rebuilding the synthetic training domain from target-domain statistics, then permitting
supervised real-domain fine-tuning.
\item[\emph{General Conclusion}.] The five research questions answered in turn, what the evidence
supports, and what follows.
\end{description}

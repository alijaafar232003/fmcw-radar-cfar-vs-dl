\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Constant false-alarm rate (CFAR) detection remains the standard approach to radar target detection,
while learned detectors increasingly report improvements over it. Such comparisons are hard to
interpret when the methods observe different inputs, use different spatial context, or are scored at
unmatched false-alarm rates --- and they rarely test whether a gain measured in simulation survives
deployment on real measurements. This thesis therefore asks not whether a learned detector beats
CFAR, but \emph{under what conditions} it does.

A \TinyParams{}-parameter convolutional detector (TinyCNN) is compared with CA-, GO-, SO- and
OS-CFAR on simulated FMCW range--Doppler maps, using comparable $\TinyRFR \times \TinyRFD$ local
context, a shared candidate-extraction and evaluation pipeline, and a matched measured false-alarm
rate. The comparison is then carried through four further stages: attribution experiments that
isolate what the learned gain is and is not; zero-shot transfer to the CARRADA automotive radar
dataset; a target-domain-informed synthetic intervention; and supervised real-domain fine-tuning.

On a fresh held-out synthetic test set the network establishes significant gains in five challenge
conditions, with homogeneous clutter a practical tie and an unresolved-target stress case showing no
significant difference after correction. Attribution shows the advantage is not simple
mainlobe-energy integration, and that the network's localisation behaviour is largely a consequence
of how it is supervised rather than an inherent limitation.

Transfer changes the picture. Evaluated zero-shot on CARRADA, the synthetic-trained network and
CA-CFAR are statistically indistinguishable in exact dense-mask recall, while the network emits
roughly \RoneBurdenRatio{} times more unmatched detections per frame. A domain audit measured large
differences between the simulator and the real sensor in background smoothness, spread, zero-Doppler
ridge structure and range non-stationarity. Rebuilding the synthetic training background from
CARRADA \emph{training}-split statistics alone --- with no real frame entering neural optimisation
--- raised exact test recall from \CiTestOrig{} to \CiTestNew{} (sequence-balanced difference
\CiTestDelta{}, 95\,\% CI [\CiTestCIlo{}, \CiTestCIhi{}]), but at roughly
\CiTestBurdenRatio{} times the output density, which burden-matched analysis showed did not
establish superiority over CA-CFAR.

Finally, fine-tuning that network on CARRADA training sequences, with the operating point fixed on
training data alone, gave exact recall \FtValFt{} against CA-CFAR's \FtValCFAR{} on previously
unscored validation sequences --- a difference of \FtDelta{} (95\,\% CI [\FtCIlo{}, \FtCIhi{}]) at a
slightly \emph{lower} output burden (\FtValBurdenFt{} against \FtValBurdenCFAR{} unmatched
detections per frame). Against the synthetic-only arm, fine-tuning established no recall gain; its
effect was selectivity, cutting output by about an order of magnitude at equal recall.

The evidence supports a conditional conclusion rather than a ranking. CFAR is the stronger
out-of-the-box detector: training-free, interpretable, and transferable without adaptation. The
learned detector has the higher observed performance ceiling, but reaches it only when the
deployment domain is represented during training.

\vspace{0.4em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} FMCW radar, CFAR detection, convolutional neural networks, domain shift,
domain adaptation.
